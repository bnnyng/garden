$\quad$**Cultural evolutionary psychology**, as proposed by [[@2019heyesCognitive]], is the view that distinctively human cognitive mechanisms are not biologically evolved instincts, but **cognitive gadgets** that arise from natural selection on *cultural variants*—traits that are transmitted through social interaction—which are acquired by individuals using a [[Domain specificity and domain generality are graded phenomena, after Laurence and Margolis|domain-general]] genetic “starter kit.”

>In a slogan: Cultural evolution shapes not just what we think but how we think it. In a saintly metaphor: Cultural evolution changes not only the grist but also the mills of the human mind (Aquinas 1272; Heyes 2012a). In a contrapuntal catchphrase: Distinctively human cognitive mechanisms – such as language, theory of mind, causal reasoning, episodic memory, imitation, and morality – are not “cognitive instincts” (Pinker 1994) but “cognitive gadgets.” These mechanisms, which are absent or merely nascent in other animals, were not designed by human minds, but they are the products of human rather than genetic agency.

> Cultural evolutionary psychology is both a framework for research and a hypothesis. As a framework, it recognises that distinctively human cognitive mechanisms can be shaped by culturally inherited information, as well as by genetically inherited information and learning (chapter 2). As a hypothesis, it proposes that cultural inheritance has played the dominant role in shaping all or most distinctively human cognitive mechanisms.

$\quad$ According to [[@2019heyesCognitive]], the **cognitive gadgets** or “special” cognitive mechanisms that humans are able to acquire from cultural environments include:

- **Imitation:** the ability of an agent to reproduce an observed behavior arises from associating the typically independent, domain-general processes of **perceptual sequence learning**—or learning to encode serial order of external stimuli—and **motor sequence learning**—or learning to encode a procedural skill;
- **Mindreading**: also called [[Theory-of-mind|theory-of-mind]]; the ability to ascribe mental states to others is learned from experts who communicate mental state concepts, and ways of representing to concepts, to novices, and can be compared to print reading/literacy;
- **Language**.
