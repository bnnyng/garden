# Instructive examples for Galois theory

#### $\mathbb Q(\sqrt 2)$

```
\mathbb Q(\sqrt 2)
```

- [[Finite extension fields]]: This extension satisfies the property of being generated by the roots a polynomial over $\mathbb Q$, since $$ \mathbb Q(\sqrt 2) = \mathbb Q (\sqrt 2, -\sqrt 2), $$ where $\pm \sqrt 2$ are all the roots of $x^2 - 2$ lying in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt 2)$.
- [[Automorphisms and polynomial roots]]: $\text{Gal}( \mathbb Q(\sqrt 2) / \mathbb Q)$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of $S_2$, which is now viewed as the set of permutations of the two-element set $\{ \sqrt 2, - \sqrt 2 \}$.

- [[(Theorem) Universal property of simple field extensions]]: Taking $F = \mathbb Q$, $E = \mathbb Q (\sqrt [3]{2})$, $K = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$, the universal property applied to the sequence $\mathbb Q \leq \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}) \leq \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ says that there is a bijection of homomorphisms $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}) \to \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ with the roots of the polynomial $$ \text{irr}(\sqrt[3]{2}, \mathbb Q) = x^3 - 2$$ in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$. There are three such roots, which are $$ \alpha_1 = \sqrt[3]{2} \quad \quad \alpha_2 = \omega \sqrt[3]{2} \quad \quad \alpha_3 = \omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2}, $$ so there are exactly three automorphisms of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ that fix $\mathbb Q$. On the other hand, consider the sequence $\mathbb Q \leq \mathbb Q(\omega) \leq \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$. If we denote the roots of $x^3 - 2$ in $\mathbb C$ are the same as above, and let $\sigma$ be [[Complex conjugation and absolute value|complex conjugation]], then $\sigma$ corresponds to the permutation $(23) \in S_3$. Notice also that $x^3 - 2$ is irreducible in $\mathbb Q(\omega)$ [^1]. Now viewing $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ as a **simple extension** of $\mathbb Q (\omega)$, the universal property implies that the homomorphisms $K \to K$—necessarily **automorphisms**—which fix $\mathbb Q (\omega)$ correspond to the roots of $x^3 - 2$ in $K$. For example, we can entirely specify an automorphism $\rho : K \to K$ by defining $$ \rho (\omega) = \omega \text{ and }\rho (\alpha_1 ) = \alpha_2 \implies \rho(\alpha_2) = \alpha_3, \ \rho(\alpha_3) = \alpha_1 \text{ as well.} $$ Indeed, $\rho$ corresponds to the **permutation** $(123) \in S_3$. All together, we have shown that $\text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q)$ has both a $2$-cycle and a $3$-cycle, so the injection $$ \rho_0: \text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q) \to S_3, \quad \quad \sigma(\alpha_i) = \alpha_{\rho_0(\omega)(i)} $$ is an **isomorphism**. 
- [[(Theorem) The number of extensions of a field homomorphism is at most the degree of the field extension]]: In the context of the theorem, let $F = \mathbb Q$ and $E = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2})$ with $[E : F] = 3$. Let $L$ be an extension field of $\mathbb Q$ which contains the three cube roots of $2$, as defined by the $\alpha_i$ above; for example, take $L = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$. Then there are three homomorphisms $\varphi : E \to L$ fixing $\mathbb Q$, but only one is an **automorphism** of $E$, i.e., $\varphi(E) = E$. ***This gives an example where $\#(\text{Gal}(E/F) < [ E : F]$ is a strict inequality; we can construct $[E: F]$ homomorphisms $E = L$, but we cannot guarantee that they will be automorphisms of $E$.***
- [[Splitting fields]]: The field $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega) = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega \sqrt[3]{2}, \omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2})$ is the **splitting field** of $x^3 - 2$ over $\mathbb Q$. #wip a product of which linear factors?
- [[(Theorem) Fundamental theorem of Galois theory]]: Let $F = \mathbb Q$ and $E = \mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ in the statement of the theorem, and identify $\text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q) \cong S_3$. We have $\alpha_3 \in E^{\langle (1 2) \rangle}$, and it follows from the fundamental theorem that $$ [\mathbb Q(\alpha_3) : \mathbb Q ] = 3 = (S_3 : \langle (1 2) \rangle) \implies E^{\langle (1 2) \rangle} = \mathbb Q(\alpha_3). $$ Similarly, $$ E^{\langle (2 3) \rangle} = \mathbb Q (\alpha_1), \quad \quad E^{\langle (1 3) \rangle} = \mathbb Q(\alpha_2). $$
- [[Separable, normal, and Galois extensions of fields]]: Since $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ is a splitting field of a nonconstant polynomial over $\mathbb Q$, namely $x^3 - 2$, we know $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)$ is a **normal**, hence **Galois extension** of $\mathbb Q$. Therefore the order of the Galois group is $$ \#(\text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q) =  [\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega) : \mathbb Q] = 6. $$ Since there is an injection $\text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q) \to S_3$ and $|S_3| = 6$ as well, so we again conclude that the **Galois group is isomorphic to $S_3$** with much less work. This means that every permutation of the roots $\{\alpha_1, \alpha_2, \alpha_3\}$ arises as some element of the Galois group, and for every $i$, there are unique **conjugate elements** $\sigma_1, \sigma_2 \in \text{Gal}(\mathbb Q(\sqrt[3]{2}, \omega)/\mathbb Q)$ satisfying $$ \sigma_1 (\alpha_1) = \alpha_i \text{ and } \sigma_1(\omega) = \omega, \quad \quad \sigma_2(\alpha_1) = \alpha_i \text{ and } \sigma_2(\omega) = \overline \omega. $$
