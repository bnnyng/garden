# Overview 

The **fundamental homomorphism theorem** is analogous to the [[(Theorem) First isomorphism theorem]] for rings, and states that every ideal arises as the kernel of a [[Ring homomorphisms and isomorphisms|ring homomorphism]].

**Principal ideals** = cyclic subgroup.

Every ideal inpolynomial ring $F[x]$ is principal, important for [[Factorization on polynomial rings|factorization]].

# Ideals of rings

>[!example] Definition: Ideal
>Let $R$ be a commutative [[Algebraic rings|ring]] with unity. A subset $I \subseteq R$ is an **ideal** if:
>- (i) $I$ is an additive [[Algebraic groups|subgroup]] of $(R, +)$;
>- (ii) **Absorbing property:** for all $a \in I$ and $r \in R$, we have $ar \in I$, i.e., $RI \subseteq I$.

>[!abstract] Lemma: Conditions for a subset to be an ideal
>A subset of a ring $I \subseteq R$ is an ideal *if and only if* $I \neq \varnothing$ is nonempty, closed under addition, and the absorbing property $RI \subset I$ holds.

# Quotient rings

>[!abstract] Proposition: A subset is an ideal iff it is a quotient ring
>If $I \subseteq R$ is an additive [[Algebraic groups|subgroup]] of the [[Algebraic rings|ring]] $R$, then [[Algebraic groups|coset]] multiplication 
>$$
>(r + I)(s + I) = rs + I
>$$
>is well-defined *if and only if* $I$ is an ideal. In this case, the additive [[Normal subgroups and quotient groups|quotient group]] $R / I$ is a ring, the **quotient ring**, under the induced operations of addition $$(r + I) + (s + I) = r + s + I $$ and multiplication as defined above for all $r, s \in R$. Further, the map $\pi : R \to R / I$ defined by $$ \pi(r) = r + I $$ is a [[Ring homomorphisms and isomorphisms|homomorphism]], the **quotient homomorphism** or **natural projection** of $R$ onto $R / I$.

- Surjective homomrophism with kernel I?

>[!abstract] Proposition (HW 4.4):
>Let $\varphi : R \to S$ be a [[Ring homomorphisms and isomorphisms|ring homomorphism]] and $I \subseteq R, J \subseteq S$ be ideals.
>- (i) The preimage $\varphi^{-1}(J) \subseteq R$ is an ideal; it follows that if $R \leq S$ is a [[Algebraic rings|subring]], then $R \cap J$ is an ideal in $R$.
>- (ii) If $\varphi$ is also [[Injectivity, surjectivity, and bijectivity|surjective]], then the image $\varphi(I) \subseteq S$ is an ideal.

# Principal ideal generated by a subset

>[!example] Definition: Principal ideal generated by a subset
>Given an element $a \in R$ of a [[Algebraic rings|ring]] $R$, the **principal ideal** generated by $a$ is the set of all “multiples” of $a$: $$ (a) = aR = \{ ra \ : \ r \in R\}. $$ 
>More generally, the ideal generated by $a_1, \ldots, a_n \in R$, is defined as the ideal $$ (a_1, \ldots, a_n) = \left \{ \sum_{i=1}^n r_ia_i \ : \ r_i \in R \right \}. $$ An ideal of the form $(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ is called a **finitely generated ideal**.

>[!abstract] Proposition: A nonzero ring $R \neq 0$ is a [[Algebraic fields|field]] *if and only if* the only ideals in $R$ are $\{ 0 \}$ and $R$.

>[!abstract] Proposition: If $F$ is a field, then every ideal in the [[Polynomial rings|polynomial ring]] $F[x]$ is principal.

*Proof from [[MATH-GU4042|Modern Algebra II]].* $\ \text{ }$ If $I = \{ 0 \}$, then clearly $I = (0)$ is principal. Otherwise, let
 $f \in I$ be a nonzero polynomial for which $\deg f$ is the minimal possible [[Polynomial rings|degree]] of polynomials in $I$ (such a choice of $f$ is possible since the set of nonnegative integers $$ \{ \deg g \ : \ g \in I, \ g \neq 0 \} $$ is a nonempty subset of $\mathbb N \cup \{ 0 \}$ and hence has a smallest element by the **well-ordering principle**). We show $I = (f)$.

Clearly, $f \in I$ implies $(f) \subseteq I$ by absorption. For the opposite inclusion, let $g \in I$. Then it follows from [[(Theorem) Long division with remainder]] that there exist $q, r \in F[x]$ with $r = 0$ or $\deg r < \deg f$ such that $g = fq + r$. Since $(f) \subseteq I$, the sum $r = g - fq \in I$ as well. Since $\deg r < \deg f$ contradicts the minimal degree assumption for $f$, we must have $r = 0$, so $g = fq \in (f)$ and we have shown $I \subseteq F$. $\ \square$

# Ideal sums and products

>[!example] Definition: Ideal sums and products
>Let $R$ be a [[Algebraic rings|commutative ring with unity]] and $I, J$ be two ideals in $R$. The **ideal sum** and **ideal product** are defined, respectively, by
>$$ I + J = \{ r + s \ : \ r \in I, \ s \in J \}, $$
>$$ IJ = \left \{ \sum_{i=1}^n r_is_i \ : \ r_i \in I, \ s_i \in J \right \}. $$

>[!abstract] Proposition (HW 4.2): 
>Let $R$ be a [[Algebraic rings|commutative ring with unity]] and $I, J \subseteq R$ be two ideals.
>- (i) The intersection $I \cap J \subseteq R$ is an ideal contained in both $I$ and $J$, and any other ideal of $R$ contained in both $I$ and $J$ is a subset of $I \cap J$.
>- (ii) The ideal sum $I + J \subseteq R$ is an ideal containing both $I$ and $J$, and every ideal of $R$ containing both $I$ and $J$ also contains $I + J$.
>- (iii) The ideal sum $IJ \subseteq R$ is an ideal contained in $I \cap J$.

#### Fields have no proper nonzero ideals

Since we require $1 \in S \leq R$ by convention for any [[Algebraic rings|subring]] $S \leq R$, a subset $I \subseteq R$ is a subring only when $I = R$:
- If $1 \in I$, then by the **absorbing property** we have $r = r1 \in I$ for all $r \in R$;
- If $u \in I$ for some [[Algebraic rings|unit]] $u \in R$, then $u ^{-1}u = 1 \in I$ as well and we have the first situation again.
It follows that [[Algebraic fields|fields]] have no **proper** nonzero ideals, i.e., every ideal of $F$ is either $\{ 0 \}$ or all of $F$.
