| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Formalism and finitary mathematics (after Hilbert) | We can only be sure of a **primitive recursive arithmetic**, i.e., the theory of finite strings of symbols on paper, much weaker than first-order [[Axioms of Peano arithmetic\|Peano arithmetic]]. Any “infinitary” mathematics, such as Cantor’s [[Naive conception of set and Russell's paradox\|set theory]], should **conservatively** extend finitary mathematics. | Requires existence of **symbol types**, but unclear how to ground these independently of their instances. Also, Gödel’s [[Undecidability and incompleteness theorems\|second incompleteness theorem]] showed that it would be impossible to prove the **consistency** of finitary mathematics in finitary mathematics. |                 |
| Intuitionism (after Brouwer, Heyting)              | Mathematics involves mental constructions, which are completely dependent on human minds.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                | Seems like a kind of selective **idealism**, e.g., are electrons mind-independent if they can only be defined using Hilbert spaces?                                                                                                                                                                                    |                 |
| Logicism (after Frege)                             |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |                 |
| Logical positivism (after Wittgenstein)            | Logical and mathematical truths are true by virtue of being the *logical consequences* of a “bare behavioristic practice, a form of life”; the truths themselves have no *descriptive content* ([[@1994putnam]]).                                                                                                                                                        | Doesn’t account for the fact that in practice, ”synthetic” results, similar to observations in empirical science, can overthrow or force modification of basic principles (e.g., deriving a contradiction).                                                                                                            |                 |

# Logicism

>[!quote] Russell (1952/2023)
>I wanted certainty in the kind of way in which people want religious faith. I thought that certainty is more likely to be found in mathematics than elsewhere. But I discovered that many mathematical demonstrations, which my teachers expected me to accept, were full of fallacies, and that, if certainty were indeed discoverable in mathematics, it would be in a new field of mathematics, with more solid foundations than those that had hitherto been thought secure. But as the work proceeded, I was continually reminded of the fable about the elephant and the tortoise. Having constructed an elephant upon which the mathematical world could rest, I found the elephant tottering, and proceeded to construct a tortoise to keep the elephant from falling. But the tortoise was no more secure than the elephant, and after some twenty years of very arduous toil, I came to the conclusion that there was nothing more that I could do in the way of making mathematical knowledge indubitable.
