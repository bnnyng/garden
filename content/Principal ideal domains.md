# Overview and basic definition

>[!definition] Principal ideal domain
>An [[Cancellation laws and integral domains|integral domain]] $R$ is a **principal ideal domain** if every ideal $I \subseteq R$ is a [[Ideals and quotient rings|principal ideal]], meaning there exists some $r \in R$ such that $I = (r)$.

**Relevant theorems:**

- [[(Theorem) A principal ideal domain is a unique factorization domain]]

# Ideals in PIDs

>[!theorem] Equivalence of maximal, prime, and principal ideal generated by an irreducible in PIDs
>Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain which is not a field, and let $I$ be an [[Ideals and quotient rings|ideal]] in $R$. Then the following are equivalent:
>- (i) $I$ is a [[Prime and maximal ideals|maximal ideal]];
>- (ii) $I$ is a [[Prime and maximal ideals|prime ideal]] and $I \neq \{ 0\}$;
>- There exists an [[Factorization on integral domains|irreducible]] element $r \in R$ such that $I = (r)$.

*Proof from [[MATH-GU4042|Modern Algebra II]].*

- **(i) $\implies$ (ii):** We know that for *any* ring $R$, if $I \subseteq R$ is a maximal ideal then it is prime ([[Prime and maximal ideals#^ba2d7f]]). By the assumption that $R$ is not a field, the ideal $\{ 0 \} \subseteq R$ is not maximal, so we know $I \neq \{ 0 \}$ as well.
- **(ii) $\implies$ (iii):** Since $R$ is a PID, by definition $I = (r)$ for some $r \in R$. Since $I \neq \{ 0\}$, we know $r \neq 0$, and irreducibility follows from the fact that if $(r)$ is prime then $r$ is irreducible in *any* integral domain ([[Unique factorization domains#^4f8b66]]).
- **(iii) $\implies$(i):** If $I  = (r)$ for irreducible $r \in R$, then since $r$ is assumed to not be a unit, we know $I \neq R$. We want to show that if $J \subseteq R$ is an ideal such that $I \subseteq J$, then $J = I$ or $J = R$. Since $R$ is a PID, we have $J = (s)$ for some $s \in R$, so the fact that $(r) \subseteq (s)$ implies $s | r$. Since $r$ is irreducible, we know that $s$ is either a unit or an associate of $r$ ([[Factorization on integral domains#^4cfae7]]). In the first case $(s) = (r)$, and in the second case $(s) = R$. Thus, $I$ is maximal. $\quad \square$

# Factorization in PIDs

>[!theorem] The GCD in a PID is a linear combination of elements
>Let $R$ be a principal ideal domain, and suppose $r, s \in R$ are not both zero. Then a [[Factorization on integral domains|greatest common divisor]] of $r$ and $s$ exists. Further, $d = \gcd(r, s)$ is a [[Linear combinations and spanning|linear combination]] of $r, s$: there exist $a, b \in R$ such that $d = ar + bs$.

>[!corollary] 
>Let $R$ be a PID.
>- (i) If $r, s \in R$ are [[Factorization on integral domains|relatively prime]] and $r | st$, then $r  | t$.
>- (ii) If $r \in R$ is [[Factorization on integral domains|irreducible]], then for all $s, t \in R$, if $r | st$ then either $r | s$ or $r | t$.
