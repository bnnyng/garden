# Overview

$\quad$ The **Benacerraf Problem** in the [[§ Philosophy of Mathematics|philosophy of mathematics]], named for Paul Benacerraf’s presentation in “Mathematical Truth,” asks how we are able to make true mathematical statements without interacting with mathematical entities ([[@2016clarke-doaneWhat]]):

> \[O]n a realist (i.e., standard) account of mathematical truth our explanation of how we know the basic postulates must be suitably connected with how we interpret the referential apparatus of the theory. \[...] [But] what is missing is precisely \[...] an account of the link between our cognitive faculties and the objects known. \[...] We accept as knowledge only those beliefs which we can appropriately relate to our cognitive faculties. (Benacerraf 1973, p. 674)

$\quad$ [[@2016clarke-doaneWhat]] argues that while the Benacerraf problem carries an important theme echoed across many philosophical domains, the problem itself is ill-defined: “It is hard to see how there could be a problem that satisfies all of the constraints that have been placed on the Benacerraf problem.”

# Interpretations of the Benacerraf problem

#### Benacerraf’s causal relation requirement 

$\quad$ For Benacerraf, “appropriately related” meant the **causal theory of knowledge**, according to which there should be a causal connection between epistemic agents and the object of knowledge:

>I favor a causal account of knowledge on which for X to know that S is true requires some causal relation to obtain between X and the referents of the names, predicates, and quantifiers of S. \[....] \[But] \[...] combining this view of knowledge with the “standard” view of mathematical truth makes it difficult to see how mathematical knowledge is possible. \[...] \[T]he connection between the truth conditions for the statements of number theory and any relevant events connected with the people who are supposed to have mathematical knowledge cannot be made out.  (Benacerraf 1973, p. 671–673)

$\quad$ Benacerraf’s own interpretation has largely been dismissed by arguments that the causal theory of knowledge should not apply to mathematical entities—including a statement from the originator of the theory, Alvin Goldman. Still, Benacerraf’s article seems to touch upon a genuine epistemological challenge for [[Mathematical realism|mathematical realism]].

> \[I]t is a crime against the intellect to try to mask the \[Benacerraf] problem \[...] with philosophical razzle-dazzle. Superficial worries about \[...] causal theories of knowledge are irrelevant to and misleading from this problem, for the problem is not so much about causality as about the very possibility of natural knowledge of abstract objects. (Hart 1977, p. 125–126)
