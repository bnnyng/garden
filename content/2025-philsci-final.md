##### 1. Compare and contrast frequentist, subjectivist, and primitivist analyses of chance.

$\quad$ Frequentism, subjectivism, and primitivism are three distinct ways to interpret chance, particularly the meaning of a probabilistic claim such as “The probability that this coin will land heads, if it is flipped, is 0.7.” For the frequentist, this claim means that the ratio of heads-outcomes to tails-outcomes will approach 7 to 3 as the number of flips approaches infinity. Probabilistic claims are therefore claims about properties of the real world, rather than, for example, personal beliefs. The main limitation of frequentism is that while it attempts to define probability in terms of observable events, this definition cannot be reduced to exclude the notion of probability. First, the claim describes a procedure of repeating coin flips that assumes the flips will be “independent and identically distributed,” which an assumption about probabilities that cannot be rephrased in terms of concrete events. Similarly, the claim about mathematical limits—a ratio as the number of flips approaches infinity—is essentially a probabilistic claim, since we cannot perform an infinite number of flips. In both these cases, a part of the frequentist definition of probability is relying on a previous understanding of chance.

$\quad$ The subjective view of probability avoids the circularity of frequentism by interpreting a statement like “The probability that this coin will land heads, if it is flipped, is 0.7” as a claim about beliefs. For the subjectivist, the meaning of such a claim is that either a rational agent (objective subjectivism) or I (subjective subjectivism, where probability values will be different for different people), would bet 70 cents to a dollar on the coin landing heads. While this definition does not depend on the notion of probability to define probability, it has another key limitation: it is not obvious why a probabilistic claim should mean something about rational agents at all.

$\quad$ Finally, the primitivist takes probability to be an irreducible logical primitive; there is no translation of probability into other concepts, and “you’ll know it when you see it.” Like the frequentist, the primitivist views probabilistic claims as claims about the external world, rather than internal beliefs. However, unlike frequentism and subjectivism, primitivism dismisses further analysis—e.g., reduction to mathematical formalism—which makes it more difficult to test in terms of observable events.

##### 2. Compare and contrast the positivist and the Instrumentalist conceptions of scientific theories.

$\quad$ Positivists take scientific theories, particularly claims about unobservable entities, to mean claims about what is directly observable (e.g., position of clocks, behavior of oscilloscopes). Under this “verificationist theory of meaning,” a scientific theory is meaningful if we can give a clear account of what kinds of observations we would need to make to decide whether the theory was true or false. The positivist view avoids general skepticism about scientific theories—how we come to know about the unobservable—and the problem of underdetermination—how to distinguish between theories that make different claims about unobservables by making the same claim about observables—by removing unobservables from scientific discourse.

$\quad$ On the other hand, for instrumentalists, scientific theories are not propositions about how the world is at all. Instead, instrumentalists conceive of scientific theories as practical tools for what will happen in the future based on what happened in the past. While both positivism and instrumentalism view observable events as the focus of science, instrumentalists view claims about unobservables are essentially ”pneumonic devices to help you remember how to do a calculation”, while positivists interpret claims about unobservables are either about directly observable procedures of predication or meaningless discourse—”grammatical illusions.” Their methods of evaluating scientific theories also differ: while positivists evaluated theories based on their truth (with respect to the observable world), instrumentalists evaluated theories based on whether they are good or effective for prediction. By avoiding the question of truth altogether, instrumentalism overcomes key challenges to positivism such as how to define observables to distinguish them from unobservables, and the infeasibility of translating theoretical terms into a finite set of empirical observations.

##### 4. Discuss the relationship between Humean attitudes towards laws and Nietzsche’s critique of scientific explanations.

$\quad$ Nietzsche critiques the epistemic privilege of scientific explanations by arguing that, in using the term “law” as a metaphor for the actions of physics, scientists are performing a “fictitious insertion of will.” Nietzsche demonstrates that all so-called governing laws are just descriptions of the world in some detail, and concludes that such a descriptivist image of laws does not truly explain anything. Instead, the notion of a law is a rhetorical device to remind people of the action of obeying, which gives rise to a feeling of having grasped some explanation. To Nietzsche, science is just another instance of a totalizing ideology in human society.

$\quad$ The characterization of scientific laws as descriptions which do not actually “govern” particular facts of the world is not a problem for Humeans, who the world as a metaphysically “flat” mosaic, or a flat totality of facts and properties in the world in four-dimensional spacetime. As a result, Humeans disavow any metaphysical distinction between laws of nature and other true propositions, viewing the true character laws as descriptive. For Humeans, the purpose of a scientific explanation is not to show why particular facts conform to nomologically necessary laws, but to clarify how a particular phenomenon fits into the entire mosaic. The Humean attitude towards laws turns Nietzsche’s argument from a critique of scientific laws *per se*, as Nietzsche himself regarded it, but a specific critique of the governing view of laws.


##### 6. What’s the best argument you know – or can think of – in favor of the claim that the natural sciences all reduce to physics? Describe that argument, and describe (as well) a way of resisting it.

$\quad$ One of the strongest arguments in favor of the claim that the natural sciences all reduce to physics is that the language and facts of all other sciences supervene on the language and facts of physics, the most fundamental science: there could not be two worlds that are psychologically different, for example, while being physically identical. Supervenience is implied by the idea that all the events that special sciences describe are also physical events, which establishes physics as the ontologically fundamental science, and opens the question of whether physics is also epistemically fundamental—that is, whether any scientific explanation can be uniquely identified with an explanation in terms of fundamental physics.

$\quad$ While the idea that all natural sciences reduce *ontologically* to fundamental physics is accepted, antireductionists such as Fodor and Putnam argue for explanatory autonomy of the special sciences, particularly psychology, on the basis of multiple realizability. A multiply realizable predicate (i.e., of a special science) is one that applies to multiple different physical states. For example, the economic predicate of “is a monetary exchange” applies to physical states corresponding to signing a check, as well as physical states corresponding to handing over cash. For Fodor, multiple realizability shows that special sciences cannot have complete explanatory dependence on physics, since the natural kinds of a special science, such as mental states in psychology, cannot correspond exceptionlessly to the natural kinds of physics, such as electrons. Fodor believes that a natural kind of a special science can only correspond to a complicated disjunction of physical predicates at best. He argues that only the former, and not the latter, expresses the kind of “true, counterfactual supporting generalization” we would like to count as scientific knowledge. In this view, the purpose of different natural sciences is to study distinct ways of carving nature into natural kinds.
