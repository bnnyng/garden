# Key terms

- **Contingent:** a (psychological) principle or law that could potentially otherwise; in contrast, a principle that is **non-contingent** is necessarily true. 
- **Ramsification:** after philosopher Frank Ramsey; the method of defining theoretical terms by their role in a system of laws.

# Reading notes

## I – Some focal problems

$\quad$ This section identifies four central problems that arise when trying to define psychological concepts—e.g., belief, desire, intention—in scientific terms. According to Grice, an adequate philosophical psychology must address these four problems.

| Problem name                                       | Main challenge                                                                                                                                                                                               | Example                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Grice’s response                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| A. Circularity in defining psychological concepts  | Defining psychological concepts in terms of behavior requires using the concept itself in the definition and/or using another psychological concept, which is subject to the same circularity problem.       |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Instead of defining psychological concepts in terms of explicit behavior, use an **axiomatic system** where concepts are **theoretical terms** (possibly primitive) governed by scientific laws. |
| B. Ambivalence in the status of psychological laws | Certain psychological principles sound both like necessary truths about rational minds (i.e., analytic) and like empirical generalizations, principles for behavior contingent on specific conditions.       | ”He who wills the end wills the means.”                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| C. Privileged access and incorrigibility           | Our first-person authority over our own beliefs and desires seems inconsistent with the empirical project of attributing beliefs and desires to people on the basis of their utility in explaining behavior. |                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| D. The selection problem                           | There many be many generalizations that involve a particular psychological concept; how do we select which laws define their meaning?                                                                        | **Optimism law:** the more one *wants* $\phi$, the more likely one is to *believe* $\phi$.<br><br>**Optimism/pessimism law:** the optimism law holds in condition $A$, but in condition $B$, the more one wants $\phi$ the less likely one is to believe $\phi$. |                                                                                                                                                                                                  |

- “To set this problem aside for a moment, the approach which I am interested in exploring is that of thinking of certain psychological concepts as theoretical concepts. They are psychological concepts just because they are the primitive concepts which belong to a certain kind of psychological theory without also belonging to any presupposed theory (such as physiological theory); and a psychological theory is a theory whose function is to provide, in a systematic way, explanations of behaviour which differ from any explanations of behaviour which may be provided by (or may someday be provided by) any presupposed theory (such as physiological theory).” (26)
	- “To explicate such psychological concepts is to characterize their role in the theory to which they primarily belong—to specify (to this or that degree of detail) the laws or quasi-laws in which they figure, and the manner in which such laws are linked.”

## II – Some general aspects of the relation between psychological theory and psychological concepts

$\quad$ To address Problem B, ambivalence between contingency and non-contingency of certain psychological laws, Grice introduces two alternative ways of introducing psychological concepts—he uses the examples of “judging” $J$ and “willing” $V$—into a formal psychological theory. Both involve **Ramsification**, or defining theoretical terms by their role in a system of laws $L$, but differ in their *metaphysical* commitment. 

| Method name          | Method definition                                                                                                                                                                         | Example                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              | Truth status of psychological principles                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| -------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ramsified naming     | There exists a *unique* instantiable entity that satisfies the description given by (a conjunction) of laws. Theoretical terms then get “assigned” as names for particular instantiables. | ”Suppose that a psychological law tells us that anyone in state $P$ hollers, and that we seek to use this law to introduce the term ‘pain.’” If we use Ramsified naming, the definition is “there is just one $P$, such that anyone in state $P$ hollers, and let us call $P$ ‘pain.’”” (28)                         | Contingent, if the underlying law is contingent; “anyone who is in pain hollers” is a statement about a property $P$ that we call pain, and therefore has the same truth status as the law “anyone in state $P$ hollers.” |
| Ramsified definition | No claim for uniqueness; terms are defined when instantiating *any* set of entities such that a conjunction of laws holds.                                                                | ”$x$ *judges* just in case there is a $J$ and there is a $V$ that $L$, and $x$ instantiates $J$; $x$ *wills* just in case there is a $J$ and a $V$ such that $L$, and $x$ instantiates $V$.”<br><br>”$x$ is in pain just in case there is a $P$ such that anyone in state $P$ hollers and $x$ is in state $P$.” (28) | Non-contingent; “anyone who is in pain hollers” is necessarily true since the definition of pain is to be “in some state which involves hollering.”                                                                       |

## III – The form of psychological “laws”

$\quad$ Grice names two potential *formal* aspects of a psychological theory that we use to define psychological terms:

- Some or all psychological laws are ***ceteris paribus***, meaning “other things being equal.” To apply these laws to an individual case, one must select the law with the most *specific* antecedent to that case.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ A system of psychological laws might have a general “head-law” that “$A$’s are $Z$”, and a subsequent “modifying law” that “$A$’s which are $B$ are $Y$,” where $Y$ is incompatible with $Z$. 
	- We can “know” these psychological laws without knowing all of their restrictive conditions, and we can include psychological laws in a larger theory without modifying the larger theory.
- Some principles are **law-allusive**, meaning they state that there exists a law of some sort without explicitly stating the law itself, often due to “a lack of quantitative expression.”
	- *Example.* $\quad$ Stating that the degrees of learning competence are correlated with stress by some function without specifying the function itself.
