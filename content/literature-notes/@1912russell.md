# Reading notes

## Scientific practice

- **(*Example*) Observation of regularities is only useful for immature sciences:** “Observation of such regularities, even when they are not without exceptions, is useful in the infancy of a science: the observation that unsupported bodies in air usually fall was a stage on the way to the law of gravitation.”
- **Nomic scientific facts are subjective and framing-dependent:** “What I deny is that science assumes the existence of invariable uniformities of sequence of this kind, or that it aims at discovering them. All such uniformities, as we saw, *depend upon a certain vagueness in the definition of the "events."*“
- **Scientific progress involves differentiation rather than unification:** “Every advance in a science takes us farther away from the crude uniformities which are first observed, into greater differentiation of antecedent and consequent, and into a continually wider circle of antecedents recognized as relevant.”
- **Antecedents that fully explain events are only useful when articulated exactly, but then they become inapplicable to other events:** “As soon as the antecedents have been given enable the consequent to be calculated with some exactitude, the antecedents have become so complicated that it is very unlikely they will ever recur. Hence, if this were the principle involved, science would remain utterly sterile.”

## Cause and effect

- **The necessity of a time interval for separating cause and effect:**
	- **A continuous cause can be chosen to be arbitrarily close to the effect:** “We shall be led to diminish the duration of the cause without limit, and however much we may diminish it, there will still remain an earlier part which might be altered without altering the effect, so that the true cause, as defined, will not have been reached, for it will be observed that the definition excludes plurality of causes.”
	- **A static cause is both physically and theoretically implausible:** “In the first place, no such cause is to be found in nature, and in the second place, it seems strange—too strange to be accepted, in spite of bare logical possibility—that the cause, after existing placidly for some time, should suddenly explode into the effect, when it might just as well have done so at any earlier time, or have gone on unchanged without producing its effect.”
- **Two main issues with the notion of cause—what is meant by an “event”, and length of the time-interval between cause and effect:**
	- **Events must recur for a causal law to be nontrivial:** “An "event" is not a particular, but some universal of which there may be many instances. It follows also that an "event" must be something short of the whole state of the universe, since it is highly improbable that this will recur.”

## Laws of probable sequence

- **(*Example*) Causal sequences of events are in fact only relatively probable, while laws necessarily hold:** “On grounds of observation, it is probable that when one occurs the other will also occur. Thus in our present sense, A may be the cause of B even if there actually are cases where B does not follow A. Striking a match will be the cause of its igniting, in spite of the fact that some matches are damp and fail to ignite.”
- **(*Example*) In mature sciences, causation is replaced by mathematical (i.e., logical) relations:** “Laws of probable sequence, though useful in daily life and in the infancy of a science, tend to be displaced by quite different laws as soon as a science is successful. The [[Newtonian mechanics|law of gravitation]] will illustrate what occurs in any advanced science. In the motions of mutually gravitating bodies, *there is nothing that can be called a cause, and nothing that can be called an effect; there is merely a formula*” (13).
- **Laws are about relations, not repetition of events:** “There is no question of repetitions, of the ‘same’ cause producing the ‘same’ effect; it is not in any sameness of causes and effects that the constancy of scientific laws consists, but in sameness of relations” (14).
- **Relational laws are precise coded by mathematical functions:** “And even "sameness of relations" is too simple a phrase; "sameness of differential equations" is the only correct phrase. *It is impossible to state this accurately in non-mathematical language*” (14).
