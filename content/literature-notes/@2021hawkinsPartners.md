# Background

> ***What are the main theories in the literature? Is there anything missing or problematic?***

- Many computational accounts of the emergence of **stable social conventions** involve groups of agents which update their language representation after each interaction, which does not address **partner-specific** or **local flexibility**.
- Theories of partner-level alignment (e.g., Donald Davidson) do not specify mechanisms for emergence of community-level [[Conventions are stable but arbitrary solutions to repeated coordination problems, after Lewis|conventions]].

# Argument and hypotheses

>***Does the article advance a new theoretical argument? If yes, what is it?***

- Three core cognitive capacities are needed for an agent to solve the computational problem of communicating in a variable, non-stationary world:
	- (C1) The ability to represent **variability** about what words will mean to different partners;
	- (C2) The ability to coordinate on partner-specific meanings via flexible **online learning**;
	- (C3) The ability to gradually generalize stable expectations about meaning based on individual interactions.
- Argument is motivated by three basic phenomena in theories of coordination and cooperation:
	- (P1) Convergence to increasingly efficient referring expressions over repeated interactions with a single partner;
	- (P2) Transition from partner-specific pacts to communal conventions expected to generalize to new partners;
	- (P3) Influence of communicative context, on which terms eventually become conventionalized.
