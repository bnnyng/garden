# Key terms

- **Reductivism:** “the view that all events which fall under the laws of any science are physical events and hence fall under the laws of physics.”
- **Bridge law:** an expression of the form $Sx \iff Px$, where $S, P$ are predicates of special sciences and physics, respectively. Different interpretations of the relation $\implies$ lead to different interpretations of the truth of reductivism, but in general bridge statements are statements of **identity**.
- **Generality of physics:** the claim that “any event which falls within the universe of discourse of special science will also fall within the universe of discourse of physics” (101); to say that physics is *the* **basic** science is to say that physics is the only science that is general in this way.

---

# Reading notes

- **Reductivism both empirical and normative:** “Though reductionism is an empirical doctrine, it is intended to play a regulative role in scientific practice. Reducibility to physics is taken to be a *constraint* upon the acceptability of theories in the special sciences, with the curious consequence that the more the special sciences succeed, the more they ought to disappear.” (97)

## Clarifying reductivism

- **Bridge laws are [[Relation|transitive]], allowing for reduction via intermediate theories:** “Whatever other properties that connective may have, it is universally agreed that it must be transitive. This is important because it is usually assumed that the reduction of some of the special sciences proceeds via bridge laws which connect their predicates with those of intermediate reducing theories. Thus, psychology is presumed to reduce to physics via, say, neurology, biochemistry, and other local stops.” (99)

| Reductivism                | Token physicalism is true *and* there are **natural kinds** in an ideal physics that correspond to natural kinds in any ideally completed special science. | Every natural kind predicate in a special science is **co-extensive** with (i.e., refers to the same set of instances as) a natural kind predicate of physics.                                  |                                                                        |
