# General definitions

#### Division

>[!definition] Division
>Let $R$ be an [[Cancellation laws and integral domains|integral domain]] and $r, s \in R$. We say that $r$ **divides** $s$ and write $r | s$ if there $s$ is a multiple of $r$, i.e., there exists $t \in R$ such that $s = rt$. Equivalently, if $(s), (r)$ are the [[Ideals and quotient rings|principal ideals]] generated by the elements $s, r$ respectively, then $s \in (r)$ and $(s) \subseteq (r)$.

💬 *Remark.* $\ \text{}$ Taking the double inclusion, it follows that for $r, s \in R$, we have $s|r$ and $r|s$ *if and only if* $(r) = (s)$.

>[!lemma] Properties of division
>- unit iff divides any other element
>- Transitive
>- sums

#### Associates

>[!definition] Associates
>Two elements $r, s \in R$ are **associates** if there exists a [[Algebraic rings|unit]] $u \in R$ such that $r = us$.

>[!lemma] [[MATH-GU4042|Modern Algebra II]] 1.2: Elements that are associates divide each other
>Given an [[Cancellation laws and integral domains|integral domain]] $R$, for all $r, s \in R$, we have $r|s$ and $s|r$ (equivalently, $(r)=(s)$) *if and only if* $r, s$ are associates, i.e., there exists a unit $u \in R$ such that $r = us$.

*Proof from [[MATH-GU4042|Modern Algebra II]].*

- ($\implies$) If $r =0$, then $r | s \iff s=0$ and so $r=1 \cdot s = 1s$, where $1$ is a unit. If neither $r, s$ is zero and they divide each other, then by definition $s = rt$ and $r = su = rut$ for some $t, u \in R$. Since $r \neq 0$ and $R$ is an integral domain, we may apply the cancellation law to obtain $1 = ut$. Then $r = su$, where $u$ is a unit.
- ($\impliedby$) If $r = us$ for some unit $u$, then $s = u^{-1}r$ and so $r,s$ are multiples of each other. $\ \square$

#### Irreducibility

>[!definition] Irreducible element
>Let $r \in R$ be neither $0$ nor a unit. We say $r$ is **irreducible** if whenever $r = st$ for some $t, s \in R$, then one of $s, t$ is a unit and the other is an associate of $r$.

#### Greatest common divisor

>[!definition] Greatest common divisor
>Let $R$ be an integral domain and let $r, s \in R$, not both zero. A **greatest common divisor** $\gcd$ of $r$ and $s$ is an element $d \in R$ such that $d | r$, $d|s$, and, whenever an element $e \in R$ divides both $r, s$, then $e | d$.

>- (i) If $d$ is a $\gcd$ of $r_1, \ldots, r_n$, then $r_1 / d, \ldots, r_n/d$ are relatively prime, i.e.,

>\gcd(r_1/d, \ldots, r_n/d) = 1.
>$$
>- (ii) If $s \in R$ is nonzero, then 
>$$
>\gcd(sr_1, \ldots, sr_n) = s \gcd(r_1, \ldots, r_n).
>$$

#### Relatively prime

>[!definition] Relatively prime
>Let $R$ be an integral domain and let $r, s \in R$, not both zero. We say $r$ and $s$ are **relatively prime** if $\gcd(r, s) = 1$; equivalently, if $d \in R$ divides both $r, s$, then $d$ is a unit (divides $1$).
