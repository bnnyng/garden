Hello Prof. Gutierrez,

I just noticed (a classmate texted me) that Canvas grades were updated, and I thought this would be a good time to share some thoughts I had on the class---which you can feel free to ignore, since this is more a train of thought that is not really directly related to your pedagogical methods :)

	First, I want to apologize for if I ever seemed flippant during our seminar. I was far out of my depth as a cognitive science major. To be honest, I initially registered for the class because I thought it would quite literally be about coding/programming (in the cognitive science major, you really have to just choose four electives and tell a cohesive narrative about them to graduate with a "specialization," and I intended to specialize in computational modeling), but I stayed with it though the add/drop period because I expected reading many neuroscience papers for the first time would be very edifying, and it was. I was particularly impressed because I entered Barnard as a psychology major, and that degree does _not_ prepare you to do psychology research; apparently the university loves to counter efforts to make it more rigorous since psychology currently functions as a catch-all degree. I feel like this was the first time I read scientific papers and did not take them at face value, i.e., I did not just read some researcher's claim, think to myself "that makes sense," and incorporate that into my worldview. I think your seminar was the first time I was able to express that kind of dissent, so I apologize if what I said was coarse or unpolished. I also vaguely remember attending a Barnard-facilitated panel that you were on in 2023, perhaps, and hearing you connect a computational model of the pendulum to neuroscientific models. In particular, I remember thinking to myself, “damn, I should network with her,” and having a lot of respect because your career trajectory was sort of a Platonic ideal for a computational neuroscientist: I once worked with Grace Lindsay at NYU, and she expressed the difficulty of learning computational methods with a purely psychological background. Also, I am not immune to physics envy (is there a way to learn about statistical physics models without taking undergrad-level mechanics and E&M? I genuinely feel some inferiority w.r.t. not being able to apply statistical physics models, e.g. Ising… I completed an internship last summer at the Santa Fe Institute. I was looking into physics master’s degrees before realizing that those typically require you to have pre-requisite knowledge of the field. I also notice that the journal club focuses on dynamics and ML, both fields I have avoided formally learning and believe I would be quite depressed). All of that is to say that being in your class is a bit of a fantasy come true.

I also want to express some admiration for my peers who, regardless of how they behaved during class (e.g., if they seemed to be motivated purely by getting good grades), impressed me with the degree of critical thinking they had for ostensibly their home discipline. I am, probably understandably, very used to people striving for the *satisficing* conditions for an A grade and nothing else. Again, I am also accustomed to psychology being a catch-all major and not actually preparing one for psychological research (apparently the university is adamant about keeping it this way? Someone has to be the scapegoat). It was certainly a new experience for me this year to concern myself with esoteric concepts like “epistemology” and “ontology.” I don’t know, these classmates just make me hopeful for the future of neuroscience (given that it has been around at Barnard for a handful of years, but when can we ever avoid the contrast between official terminology for funding and real science. It seems that whenever Barnard applauds neuroscience, it is within the scope of how wonderful it is to have women care about STEM). My general perspective is that it is challenging to do a neural science well, hence me hiding in the behavioral sciences (in particular, I can always validate a behavioral theory by just thinking really hard about myself, lol. This is how I received an A+ in so-called Social Neuroscience this semester. My deepest apologies to Jon Freeman because I am, to some degree, envious of his professional success, but upon reflection, I do not think I would be pleased with a career built from fMRI results.) Not sure what my point is here; I guess if any of them request recommendations from you sometime in the future, trust that they care a lot about doing science well?
