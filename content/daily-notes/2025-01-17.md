#### Describe an activity or experience that has been important in helping shape or reinforce your desire to pursue a research career in science, mathematics or engineering. (1500 Characters)

In Ted Chiang’s novella “The Lifecycle of Software Objects,” digital entities sold as virtual pets gradually develop intelligence by interacting with humans, forcing their owners to confront the nature of agency, experience, maturity, and consent. 

I was surprised to find Chiang giving a talk on this story, which had cemented him as my favorite author years ago, on the my first day as a research intern at SFI. His thesis permanently altered my understanding of cognition, my research passion: life experience, he argued, is incompressible; the only way to engineer intelligence is via artificial life. Fiction had seemed orthogonal to “real” science, but Chiang’s presentation showed their mutuality: inspired by scientific discourse, his stories synthesize and amplify informal theories to spark new research directions. 

 Before, my engagement with actual research communities was limited to occasional lab meetings. At SFI, I lived with scientists full-time. I was even spoken to as a colleague, thanks to SFI’s emphasis against hierarchy. The interdisciplinary culture exposed me to more new ideas in 10 weeks than I had learned in the previous 10 months; a given discussion could span topics from the multiverse to Bali’s ten-dimensional calendar. Developing my own project in this environment carried the thrill of Chiang’s storytelling, packaged in a legitimate research program.
