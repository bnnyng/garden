# Notes

#### [[PHIL-UN3551|Philosophy of Science]]

- What kind of necessity is involved in a scientific law?
	- Logical (no scientific laws are logically necessary)
	- Logical = contradiction would be involved if it was false; puts limits on what we imagine
		- We can easily imagine what the world would be like if 
	- Also not contingently true
- Terms: physically necessary?
- Nomic necessity: list of jobs
	- Role in explanation
	- Logical entailment $\neq$ restatement – what else do we want from an explanation?
- Albert: two features
	- Restatements don’t cause gaining understanding
	- More that it simply did happen, but given other conditions, must have happen
		- Some conceptual distance, fitting into larger pattern of the world
		- More remote = more satisfying
- “Apparently a feature” for gnomically (not just contingent) can be exploited to support counterfactual claims
	- Counterfactual claim = “had \[false antecedent] been the case, \[something] would have happened”
		- “If I hadn’t knocked over the ink, there wouldn’t be a stain on the ground”
		- “If I had dropped my glasses, they would have accelerated toward the earth at 9.8 m/$s^2$”
		- Counterfactuals appeal to facts of life/regularities that have “nomic force”
	- Open question/recurring theme: how can people think they know the truth value? By hypothesis, they happen in alternate worlds!
		- What does it mean?
- “Three jobs that we will want to explain” (see Goodman; the third job)
	- Propositions that are “confirmable by their instances”
		- Our confidence goes up really fast after seeing a small number of positive inferences
		- In contrast: “Are there any volcanic rocks that are shaped by Mickey Mouse?” — would be crazy to conclude there were no such rocks, since it seems like a contingent fact; NOT a candidates for nomic fact
	- **Certain propositions feel like candidates for nomic fact**
- Eliminated: generality
- Counterfactual = relation between antecedent and consequence?
- “So powerfully true that they spill over to neighboring worlds”
- Modal statements = claims about how things would be in
- What is going on? – David Armstrong, Laws of Nature
	- World has a level of particular facts. Hovering above particulars in Platonic heaven, are the Platonic forms—”real objects”
		- Example: property or form of redness, vs. particular red things (red in virtue of participating in the Platonic red)
	- Nomic truths at the particulars are there because of contingent relations between forms
		- Example: President is the boss of VP. Why? Not because of relation between individuals, but relation between positions/offices.
		- “everything with property F has G”
	- There can be contingent facts among particulars
	- Hence it could not have been otherwise
	- Answer to how to get something between contingent and logical truths? Nomic truths at particulars is a contingent truth among forms
		- The “necessity relation between F and G”
	- Read armstrong and then read Van Frassen, “Laws and Symmetry”
- Primitivism = “you know it when you see it. No analysis”
- Governance image of laws?
	- “Law” is metaphorical, has pre-scientific meanings (civil law); the metaphor means that there are certain ways that the world just “happen to be” and others that are “commanded” to be true by that which governs the world 
	- Question: How to be literal about it?
	- “De facto” truth that I don’t (stand on my head for 10 hours at a time); nomic claim that I don’t drive at 100 mph on the freeway
	- Conveys image of obeying a soverign
- Hume’s revolt – descriptivism
	- Billiard ball goes in and another goes out, and everyone says I have to see the third thing (going through) by necessity
- See Nietzsche on Science in The Will to Power
	- Retreats toward a purely descriptivist attitude towards science
	- Hence no insight that science claims to have
	- Always uses metaphors to conveying the feeling that something has been explained rather than described: “fictitious insertion of a will”; subconscious psychological phenomena
		- The feelings go away after the metaphors are disavowed
	- Critique of 19th century scientists
