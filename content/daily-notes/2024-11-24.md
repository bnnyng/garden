When thinking about mathematics, most people, including my high school self, imagine a purely cognitive challenge, perhaps remembering their experience with a particularly difficult problem or remarking, unprompted, that they just “can’t do math.” Because I could not understand it, I remained largely uninterested in it until I impulsively took a proof-based calculus course in college. Only then did I realize that mathematics is not merely a collection of disembodied, abstract reasoning tasks, but an unavoidably social practice: a mathematical concept exists to the extent that it can be communicated between minds, whether teacher-to-student or researcher-to-researcher.

Studying math has complemented my long-standing interest in human cognition and inspired several new research questions.
