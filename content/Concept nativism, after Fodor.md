According to J. Fodor, learning a **concept**—the building blocks of propositions—involves forming and testing a hypothesis about what that concept means. Such hypotheses are formed by compounding more primitive concepts.

Fodor’s theory of **concept nativism** resolves the issue of where the most primitive concepts—that is, the so-called **unanalyzable** concepts, which cannot be further decomposed into meaningful semantic structure—come from by proposing that primitive concepts are *automatically activated* by experience, not created from it. That is, most concepts (e.g., object permanence) are innate, and simply require a causal process to “come online.” (Hence Fodor refers to all of developmental psychology as operating a stopwatch.) 

This theory presumes that the concepts are the same for everyone, or at least [[Categories and morphisms|isomorphic]] to one another.

From [[@1998cowie]]:
>What makes a concept unlearnable (hence unlearned) is features intrinsic to the concept itself—its lack of internal semantic structure, its unanalysability. … Most concepts are like CAUSE and unlike BACHELOR: they firmly resist our efforts to provide them with non-circular definitions.
