# Discussion post

I understood [[@2019heyesPrecis]]’s basic argument to be that higher cognitive functions, particularly those attributed uniquely to humans, are “gadgets” based on domain-general learning procedures and specified by selection on cultural variants. As Heyes’ reponse clarifies, cognitive gadgets lie on a continuum opposite cognitive instincts, which arise from selection on genetic variants. Gadgets are also distinct from a “genetic starter kit” that includes being more tolerant and socially motivated, a predisposition to paying more attention to other humans, associative learning, and executive function. While Heyes’ discussion mostly focuses on advancing the cultural selection hypothesis, to me, the causal link between this starter kit and the emergence of cognitive gadgets is the most interesting (and weakest) part of the theory; that is, rather than determining proportions of nature, nature, and culture involved in enabling cognitive mechanisms, but what nature underlies the nurture and culture.

I focused on Sperber’s response since his co-authored book [[@2017mercier]] is a central source for my final paper. I am particularly sympathetic to [[Neural expatation|neural expatation]] as an explanation for the neural substrates of various culturally-relevant skills; for example, [[@2005dehaene]], which Heyes also cites, showed that the skill of reading recruits the left occipitotemporal sulcus, which also helps perception of object contours. How should we determine where reading falls on the continuum, then? More precisely, if the two selection types, genetic or cultural, are discrete, how do we establish a continuous range of traits—by proportion? Something about this quantification feels arbitrary. 

It might be more appropriate to have a hierarchical taxonomy: all cultural traits have a biological substrate, and the question is not how much of a cognitive mechanism is explained by cultural vs. genetic selection, but whether selection on cultural traits is sufficient to explain cognitive mechanisms. The nature vs. nature debate makes sense for characterizing individual behavior, but I struggle to see how any account of culturally-determined cognitive mechanisms does not ultimately depend on strictly biologically determined abilities and predispositions, beyond a “starter kit.” I don’t think I disagree with Heyes per se (I’m still parsing how exactly her and Sperber disagree) and I quite like the metaphor of cognitive gadgets, but the gadget-instinct continuum is conceptually muddy to me.
