# Discussion post

The discussion of transformational abstraction reminds me of the line of [representational](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.09.031) [geometry](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07799-x) research in computational neuroscience, which similarly looks to characterize the manifolds in feature space spanned by firing rates of neural populations (where each dimension is a feature, each neuron is a vector, and the value of a vector in a given dimension is the neuron’s firing rate in response to that feature). In this case, **abstraction** is defined as the compression of relevant structure that is shared across task contexts (by participants in these studies), and an **abstract representation** is a manifold that is linearly separable. And there are many reasonable results from these studies, such as a positive correlation between measures of abstraction and successful performance on behavioral tasks. 

Unfortunately, this similarity between Buckner’s proposal (specifically, using these [manifolds](https://colah.github.io/posts/2014-03-NN-Manifolds-Topology/) to reveal something about perceptual abstraction) and an established neuroscience research program makes me *more* hesitant about its utility for understanding human cognition, in particular because I wrote a substantive paper last semester about how I didn’t think these methods helped us understand human cognition. For example:

>Although the studies typically refer to the geometric structure of neural activity as a “representational geometry,” it is unclear whether this refers to an ontological claim about how particular cells work, or simply the method of representing neurons using geometry. … The methods alone cannot guarantee that neurons actually perform some process of generating manifolds and then linearly separating them. 

It seems to me that applying insights from DNNs to another “level of computation”, so to speak, would run into similar issues while being an even harder comparison to justify. Like, maybe early perceptual transformations are highly mathematical because our physical world is “up to its ears” in mathematical descriptions, but it is unclear to me if this suffices to generate every concept we use in thought.
