I found Brette (2015) invaluable for understanding the basis of the debate between rate and temporal codes. Of course, it was also convenient for me that the author supported a temporal, or spike-based, code. At many points in this course, I have brought up my confusion about why mathematical constructs (e.g., tuning curves, firing rate, representational geometries) used to describe neural activity—that is, epistemological claims about which measurement is more appropriate—are being discussed as if they reflect some objective truth about how information processing is performed by neurons—an ontological claim. I suppose how problematic this is depends on how much pluralism you accommodate and realism you require in your models: if you just want your model to be good at prediction, then maybe you don’t care about ontology at all. But this perspective from a researcher would be surprising.

I remember in particular asking why “the debate between rate and temporal codes is even a debate,” since it is incredibly obvious that firing rate is based on spike timings, so spike timings must matter. Brette (2015) clarified for me that the debate is fundamentally about whether firing rates are simply a construct used by researchers, or if they play a causal role in how neurons “encode” information. 

Further, when evaluating the predictive abilities of rate vs. temporal codes (clearly I do not think that predictive ability is sufficient for showing a scientific model is *ontologically valid*, but I can accept that it’s necessary), I found it interesting that the Seth (2015) summary kind of glossed over the fact that a spike-based code had better predictive accuracy over a rate-based code. This suggests that even as a measurement construct, rate codes are not always a reliable coarse-graining. Given that individual neurons are incredibly complicated, it is just not plausible to me that, in general, their activity can be reduced to rates in the same way that the motion of individual molecules can be reduced to temperature, for example.

I think the fragility of these epistemological-disguised-as-ontological claims became apparent to those of us who attended Randy Gallistel’s talk after class last Wednesday. In the talk, as in Gallistel (2009), he claims that information is not encoded (that is, memory is not stored) in synapses, but in molecules (e.g., nucleotides), since associative bonds do not store information in a format that makes it available for processing. The upshot of this (highly controversial, I’m sure) theory is that neither spike trains nor firing rates would indicate much about information processing at all, since molecular encoding would take barely perceptible amounts of energy; the fact that such a theory is even plausible implies that the idea that neural activity encodes information is a result of the fact that we think it is methodologically valid to record neural responses to determine what is being processed. If we have the time, I think that some of us would be very eager to briefly discuss this theory and potential implications for how to think about the neural code in general!
