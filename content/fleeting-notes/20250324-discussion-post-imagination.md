# Discussion post

I feel like “imagination” remains somewhat ill-defined. Buckner makes a compelling case for GANs and VAEs being “proofs of concept” for deep neural nets being capable of a particular kind of mental activity, the ability to produce exemplars from an experienced distribution and to construct a latent space of feature clusters—the “novel composite categories.” The way to determine that DNNs have this particular imaginative capacity, funnily enough, is a return to behaviorism: to inspect the exemplars the network produces in order to confirm that it has semantically coherent representations. 

I was confused by whether the latent space theory of representations held up for the “pink elephant in a tutu” and other novel composite categories, since DALL-E is a transformer that does not have a *continuous* latent space (i.e., affords interpolation). Does discrete-continuous distinction suggest that making novel composite categories and sampling exemplars from experienced distributions should be treated as separate faculties, rather than both being subsumed under the faculty of “imagination”?

Anyway, it seems to me that many ordinary imaginative acts lie beyond the scope of the particular mechanism described in this chapter. In fact, I find it hard to understand imagination at all without some sort of [teleological story](https://philarchive.org/rec/GOLIAA-4), which DNNs lack. (Incidentally, Goldwasser’s thesis that imagination is a skillful act is very aligned with the empiricist program!) Imagination is most often employed to transcend the limits of physical bodies (e.g., counterfactuals, pretense, mind-reading/theory-of-mind). The discussion so far hasn’t addressed a very basic feature of human imagination, which is the capacity to make representations at will and in the absence of direct stimuli. Our current models are proofs of concept, but what concept are they proving exactly?
