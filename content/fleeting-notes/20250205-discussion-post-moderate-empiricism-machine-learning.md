# Discussion post

I assume the following passage summarizes Buckner’s view in 1.1–1.3, emphasis mine:

> “The most productive way to construe the debate, particularly in the context of the dispute over the future of AI, concerns only the number and domain specificity of the innate concepts and learning systems that must be manually programmed into the mind to achieve *human-like learning and cognition* (18).

This seems to support the “existence proof” view of AI’s contributions to cognitive science, and thus, doesn’t fully refute Marcus’s point (I think, certainly defer to Buckner on this though) that human-like AI systems will always be behavioral approximations—if only in virtue of the fact that we *have* no fact of the matter for what is “programmed” into humans. Even if our AI systems replicated human behaviors to a satisfying degree, what kind of knowledge would we have gained? What precisely did we explain? (Open questions!)

I have increasingly been thinking of cognitive science, and complexity science more generally, as a meta-theory of cybernetics and control flow. On that theme, I recently read Barron et al. (2023)’s intentionally provocative paper, “Transitions in cognitive evolution,” which argues that such transitional states should be partitioned by the phylogenetic possibilities at each stage instead of specific cognitive capacities. There is some flavor of the nativism-empiricism debate at the control level: in the most computationally complete tier, which the authors call “reflective architectures,” the innate ability of a system to reprogram itself enables a sort of “blank slate” in the form of virtual machines. Will have to think more to justify the connection to this class, but at the very least, a description at the level of cybernetics gives alternative way to classify the kind of “cognition” AI systems have.
