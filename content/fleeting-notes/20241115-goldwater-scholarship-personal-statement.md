# Campus pre-application

When thinking about mathematics, most people, including my high school self, imagine a purely cognitive challenge, perhaps remembering their experience with a particularly difficult problem or remarking, unprompted, that they just “can’t do math.” Because I could not understand it, I remained largely uninterested until I impulsively took a proof-based calculus course in college. I realized then that mathematics is not just a collection of disembodied facts and formulas, but a dynamic practice ripe with insights for my long-standing interest in human cognition.

Learning math has inspired several research questions that I may not have considered otherwise. For example, what causes the gestalt switch from merely being able to apply theorems to seeing them as necessarily true? Which cognitive capacities allow humans to intuitively leap to conclusions that our state-of-the-art AI models can only prove via brute-force search? What makes explanations convincing, and what does this suggest for teaching effectively? 

My primary career goal is to research the nature of human learning and problem-solving, whether with mathematics as my testbed or not, and design intelligent systems that complement these activities.

My uncommon combination of majors in mathematics and cognitive science uniquely position me for this research. Advanced math classes provide first-hand intuition for promising theories and a strong quantitative background for designing rigorous cognitive models, which I will apply directly in my year-long senior thesis in Computational Cognitive Science. To prepare, I designed a specialized curriculum that reflects cutting-edge methods, including Theoretical Neuroscience and Bayesian Statistics. I have also sought research experience in modeling at all levels: convolutional neural networks for early vision, Gaussian processes for the mental processes of teaching/learning, and information theory for explicit descriptions of reasoning processes. I hope that completing the thesis will also clarify what methods I want to emphasize in future work.

Between each undergraduate year, I participated in funded summer programs that prepared me for graduate school and academia both explicitly and implicitly. At the Santa Fe Institute, I was taught to pitch my project to potential advisors and give talks to an interdisciplinary research community, and I learned indirectly that I prefer frequent meetings with an accessible advisor while conducting projects relatively independently. I am working with my SFI mentors to submit our results to the Cognitive Science Conference, providing experience in long-distance co-authoring. This exposure to different mentoring and institutional dynamics equips me with a realistic understanding of research careers beyond projects and coursework.

I plan to pursue a Ph.D. in Cognitive Science immediately after graduation, with specific program depending on my preferred approach---e.g., resource-rational Bayesian models (MIT, Princeton), program synthesis for abstract reasoning (NYU), “classical” cognitive psychology (Indiana University, Stanford). I hope to return to SFI as a Complexity Postdoctoral Fellow, since the non-hierarchical, non-departmental culture and regular visitors will be invaluable for early-career ideation and networking. Eventually, I hope to conduct full-time research as a cognitive science professor, and I envision my work finding applications in AI and educational technology, as well as my own teaching.
