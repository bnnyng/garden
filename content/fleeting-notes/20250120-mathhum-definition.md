Bonnie Yang<br>January 21, 2025

Mathematics is a culturally developed body of knowledge for which ground truth is the set of valid statements agreed upon by a far-flung future community of mathematicians that share the same fundamental assumptions. Like Conway’s Game of Life, given a set of initial conditions (i.e., axioms), there exist mathematical statements at “equilibrium” (i.e., accepted as true by most practicing mathematicians; analogous to patterns like the “glider”), as well as other concepts in flux. Different mathematical cultures can be considered to have different initial states, and most of mathematics emerges from local behavior (i.e., discoveries) of individual agents.

At the level of individual cognition, “intuitive” knowledge of mathematics is represented in a *world model* that includes both the referents of formal propositions and the causal relations between them (e.g., when a theorem can be applied to bridge two concepts). This captures the fact that reasoning about mathematics can be both *theoretical*, where explicit premises are “taken” to support a conclusion, and *practical*, where a conclusion is arrived at on the basis of beliefs and desires; the latter reasoning process is often not representable in formal logic [^1].

[^1]: Definitions of theoretical and practical inference from doi: 10.1111/phpr.12455.
