# Discussion post

Contrary to Buckner’s remark on page 75 about Turing’s limited discussion of “intelligence”, Turing’s 1948 article “Intelligent Machinery”—which I just happened to have as assigned reading for MathHum this week—is a fairly in-depth investigation of cognitive (computational) capacities that seems relevant to the learned vs. innate knowledge debate. The paper focuses on “unorganized machines” (neural networks) and the feasibility of interfering with them—i.e., modifying their behavior by changing instructions alone, as the other sort of interference involving replacing and removing physical parts is nearly trivial—such that they become “universal logical computing machines.” He thinks that many insights can be gleaned from human development, toward which he takes a largely empirical stance:
- Humans have their mental patterns induced by experience: “At the end of the period a large number of standard routines will have been superimposed on the original pattern of his brain. These routines will be known to the community as a whole. He is then in a position to try out new combinations of these routines, to make slight variations on them, and to apply them in new ways.”
- The human cortex is an unorganized machine: “The difference between the languages spoken on the two sides of the Channel  is not due to difference in development of the French-speaking and English-speaking parts of the brain. It is due to the linguistic parts having been  subjected to different training. We believe then that there are large parts of  the brain, chiefly in the cortex, whose function is largely indeterminate.”
- Echoing DoGMA, the process of interfering with an unorganized machine might reveal something about human cognition: “The resemblance to a universal machine is still  very great, and suggests to us that the step from the unorganized infant to a  universal machine is one which should be understood. When this has been mastered we shall be in a far better position to consider how the organizing  process might have been modified to produce a more normal type of mind.”
- Cognitive abilities are not differentiated by innate biology: “The possession of a human cortex (say) would be virtually useless if no attempt was made to organize it. Thus if a wolf by a mutation acquired a human  cortex there is little reason to believe that he would have any selective advantage.”

He also appears to introduce the notion of genetic algorithms, as well as both biological and human cultural evolution as forms of intelligent (heuristic) search. Nothing new under the sun, etc.

Obviously the sort of research I’m doing assumes a (resource)-rational paradigm, so I shan’t complain about that. My one qualm with applying this analysis to deep learning models (e.g., Buckner’s Tiers of Rationality on page 83) rather than human subjects is that, by large, we have good reason to assume that humans pursue goals of their own volition. Turing also comments on this as a distinct faculty from learning: “To convert a brain or machine into a universal machine is the extremest form of **discipline**. … But discipline is certainly not enough in itself to produce intelligence. That which is required in addition we call **initiative**. … Our task is to discover the nature of this residue as it occurs in man, and to try and copy it in machines.” Does the DoGMA strategy take the existence of a goal for granted?

Finally, it is also interesting that social decision-making is an “icing on the cake” for Buckner. Assuming that this means something like coordination with like agents, this is a basic phenomenon of the physical world, so I wonder why this is not prioritized as a feature of rationality.
