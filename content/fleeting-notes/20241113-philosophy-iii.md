# Discussion post

[[@2005smith]] gave a compelling case for the importance of embodiment, but not the claims of anti-representationalism it was sandwiched between. I broadly agree with the embodiment hypothesis: accounts of cognition based on mental states alone are inadequate, and understanding the relationship between cognition and environment requires analytical tools from complexity sciences, like networks and dynamical systems. However, I think that representations are necessary even in some of the examples given: in order for self-edifying experimentation to happen, a system (e.g., infant) must have some sense of planning; if planning is entirely contained in interactions with the world, how does that capacity generalize to nonphysical problems? In the case of mother-infant coupled behavior, do they not need to share a model of the pragmatics of their vocalizations and expressions? Such representations certainly emerge from embodied dynamics, but then they should be studied seriously along with other emergent properties like stabilities and instabilities (is a representation not just a stability?). I felt like [[@2005smith]] was implying an ontological objection to representations, while the argument was epistemological: “As theoretical constructs, they have reality only if they are necessary to explain phenomenon.”
