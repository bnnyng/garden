## Post

My position towards the functionalism debate is entirely captured by this passage toward the end of [[@2022cao]]: “If it is possible to specify the functional roles and organization at an *informative* level of detail that is nonetheless abstract enough to be tractable, then we will have specified an explanatory and predictive model of the phenomenon of interest.” This is perhaps a lower standard for a satisficing theory than what we’ve discussed in this class, but I am interested in “empirical” cognitive science research, and I think anything stronger would make acting on that interest impossible. To think that such an activity is worthwhile at all, I either have to believe that such a thing as “cognition” or “mentality” exists outside of human constructs—and I see no reason why it should—or I have to believe that a cognitive level of description is useful in some way (e.g., to characterize a specific class of regularities in the world that we care about). Why should nature have distinguished “joints” for us to carve? I always end up talking about math in these discussion posts, but it is such an interesting human activity: mathematical objects are arguably more thoroughly understood than things outside of us in nature, and there are still a plurality of explanations/perspectives for many of them.

As for actual content of the reading, I agree fully with Cao’s skepticism of the neuron doctrine and the premise that “MR at the neural level entails MR at higher levels,” but I do not see why the converse should be true: “The kind of causal structure necessary for a mind like ours can only be satisfied by a body like ours and a brain like ours.” But by the previous paragraph, my concept of “like ours” is more arbitrary than it probably should be. 

A few more thoughts:
- “How many biochemical mechanisms can we dispense with? … We still need sensors.” I actually think that our senses are a key determinant of our mental states. Most directly, our perceptual structures distill the world into information our minds can do more things with. In a previous post, I mentioned the idea of sensory matched filters: animals with limited energy resources, such as blowflies, develop sensory structures that are highly “matched” to the environment, thus taking on a greater share of the information-processing burden.
- “Highly domain-specific capacities are multiply realizable.” I think this idea can be applied to realizing behaviors that require “higher cognition.” Related to the notion of perceptual filtering, I notice that many cognitive tasks can be decomposed into a domain-specific language and a domain-general algorithm over that language (regardless of if this is how human minds go about it). For example, the success of AlphaProof depended on the behavior, human mathematical practice, having a structure that was easy to formalize into a proof language like Coq. Similarly, I wonder if human performance on visual abstract reasoning tasks (e.g., ARC) is somehow mediated by the “pre-processing” done by the human visual system.
- Cao uses the term “constraints” like how I might use “parameters.” It could be interesting to consider how humans are over- or under-parametrized with respect certain tasks.
- The notion of “generative entrenchment,” where certain functions become dependent on initially unrelated parts of the brain due to them just being “available,” reminds me of Cormac McCarthy’s essay “[[@2017mccarthy|The Kekulé Problem]],” where he argues that language propagated itself virally in pre-existing evolved brain structures. “Generative entrenchment” could be an interesting lens to view the necessity of language in our (collective/cultural) memory and ongoing cognition.
