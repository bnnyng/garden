# Hume’s argument against induction

**See also:** [[2025-philsci-final-review]]

$\quad$ Hume argues that inductive inferences presuppose the **principle of uniformity of nature**—the thesis that things we have not experienced should resemble things we have experienced—which we have no basis to expect. Hume specifically focuses on inductive inferences of the following form: “All observed instances of $A$ have been $B$. The next instance of $A$ will be $B$.”

$\quad$ Some philosophers have divided Hume’s problem into the **descriptive problem**, or describing how inductive reasoning works in individual minds, and the **justificatory problem**, or why it is rational to believe inductive inferences (in particular, despite lacking a deductive justification). Hume himself answered the descriptive problem, believing that an inductive inference “taken by the mind” is induced by the principle of habit.

| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Nelson Goodman | **Descriptive problem:** how to distinguish between inductive generalizations that we consider “law-like” or “confirmable by instances”, and accidentally true generalizations. <br><br>**Justificatory problem:** Following Hume, there is no deductive, logical connection between past/present spacetime regions and future/unobserved regions.            | **Descriptive problem:** “Law-like” statements include predicates that have been successfully projected in by past by our epistemic community, and distinguished by accidentally true generalizations by their “special confirmability by their instances.”<br><br>**Justificatory problem:** There is no “external, absolutely firm place” to justify inductively obtained scientific knowledge. |
| Karl Popper    | Popper takes the “standard” reading of Hume’s observation—that it is about lack of deductive justification.<br> <br>**Justificatory problem:** Induction cannot be justified since the only legitimate kind of inference is by means of logical deduction, and (after Hume) there are no strictly logical connections between different regions of spacetime. | **Justificatory problem:** Popper sees an asymmetry between verification and falsification: while a general law cannot be deductively verified from observations, a general law can be falsified if an observation contradicts what the law deductively implies.                                                                                                                                  |

# Quotations

- **John Stuart Mill (1843; quoted in [[@2024griffithsBayesian]], p. 6):** “Why is a single instance, in some cases, sufficient for a complete induction, while in others, myriads of concurring instances, without a single exception known or presumed, go such a very little way toward establishing a universal proposition? Whoever can answer this question knows more of the philosophy of logic than the wisest of the ancients, and has solved the problem of induction.”
