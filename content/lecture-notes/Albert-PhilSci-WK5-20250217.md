**Causation as core knowledge**

- **Locality** is our innate intuition—in particular, expectations—for how causal relations in the world work.
	- The “chain reaction” of relations from initial cause to final effect has no spatial or temporal breaks. That is, we assume that causation happens between **spatially and temporally adjacent** items.
	- Effects can build up to spatially and temporally remote impacts, but only through a **mediating chain** of physical, observable processes.
- Locality is a deep form of **core knowledge**; non-human animals such as fish have it.
- Conversely, **magic** refers to two different events that are said to have a causal relation but lack a traceable process of propagation between them.

**Locality’s success in early modern physics**

- Violations of locality in early modern physics were characterized as “**actions at a distance**”; non-locality was a reason to be skeptical of physical theories.
	- *Example.* $\ \text{}$Newton’s [[Newtonian mechanics|law of gravitation]] was initially dismissed due to the non-locality of the theory, even by Newton himself. Newton’s notebooks include attempts to form an **explanation** of gravitation that only involves with local particles.
- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ In the 19th century, the notion of **electric fields** carried by charged particles began as a **notational innovation**. The notation implies a local relation: rather than charged particles interacting at a distance, phenomena can be explained in terms of their adjacent fields.
	- By the end of the 19th century, “the basic ontology of physics—the physical furniture of the universe—had doubled,” and people had become persuaded that fields were real, physical things in the world, just like material particles.
- Why was the theory of electric and magnetic fields persuasive?
	- People were **invested** in the principle of the conservation of energy. Maxwell’s equations suggested that the total energy associated with the interaction of particles alone would not be conserved.
	- Maxwell further discovers a **simple formula** for associating energy with electromagnetic fields. Assuming this formula was correct—that is, the existence of fields—one can prove the very general result that the total energy of both particles and fields is conserved.
	- Further, to make accurate **predictions** about particles, one needs information about the **initial*** configuration of fields in addition to positions and velocities of the particles themselves. This suggests that otherwise, the physical initial state is missing information.
	- Maxwell’s equations implied that fields had **rich internal dynamics** of their own—even in a situation where there no material particles in the world. This suggests that the existence of fields is not **ontologically parasitic** on the existence of particles.
	- Maxwell’s equations predicted a **characteristic velocity** for the propagation of electromagnetic fields that coincided with the recently discovered **velocity of light**.
- Upshot: Maxwell’s equations **restored locality** (after two hundred years) by giving a completely local theory of the previously remote interactions between electrostatic particles characterized by Coulomb.
	- Further, Einstein’s theory of gravitation, **general relativity**, realized his ambitions of localizing gravitational interactions in the same way that Maxwell’s equations localized electrostatic interactions.

**Non-locality and quantum mechanics**

- Among other reasons, Einstein’s discomfort with the theories of **quantum mechanics** came from their non-locality, which suggested that, as in the cases of gravitation and electrostatic particles, the theory was **incomplete**.
- In 1964, Bell proves that the **predictions** of quantum theory (about yet unimplemented experiments that he suggested) could not be replicated by any local theory.
	- Later, the experiments produced results that could only have been the outcome of non-local quantum theory, confirming Bell’s argument **on the basis of logic**.
- Upshot: locality is **false** in our world. Bell derived a contradiction, one horn was proven empirically, and hence the other horn must be false.
