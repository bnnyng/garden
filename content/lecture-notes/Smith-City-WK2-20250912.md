## Lecture: A brief history of urban design

>[!example] Takeaways
>- There are two general approaches to urban design in tension: principles inspired by traditional and organic city forms, and principles that attempt to prescribe or orient contemporaneous life.
>- Urban designers often take themselves to be apolitical, but city planning has widespread consequences for social, economic, and political life.


**Pre-modern and medieval city form**

- “Urban design” is a modern invention, but this does not mean cities lacked design—city form was a function of a social, economic, and political life.
	- **Vernacular design (and architecture):** buildings and spaces that are not designed by professionals, but emergent from civilian lifestyles; “traditional” or “organic” urban design is a *vernacular* practice.
		- **Lines of desire:** the emergence of trails and roads based on where people walk.
- The spatial development of pre-modern cities was guided by the following general principles: civil power structures, modes of transportation, ease of expansion.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ **San Gimignano, Italy:** city form is defined by towers, which reflect the dominance of powerful families.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ **Cologne, Germany:** organized around docks; narrow, winding streets due to constraints on space (nor do they need to be wide, since they only need to accommodate pedestrians and carts).
	- *(Counter?) example.* $\quad$ **Beijing, China:** well-defined walls around the **Forbidden City**, with urban development outside the walls (and additional walls added to accommodate expansions); expansions tend to happen around gates of access.

		- Chinese cities were also guided by the principles of **feng shui**, which designs a city in relation to the landscape and cardinal directions.


**Origins of modern urban design**

- **Camillo Sitte (1880s–1890s):** “grandfather” of modern urban design, motivated by the concerns of European city form in the ; the first to codify “universal” principles of city form in medieval/pre-modern cities that he sees as reflecting “organic” life.
	- Particularly focused on the study of plazas.
- In opposition to city design as a *technical practice* aimed at resolving social and health concerns, Sitte sees city design as a *creative undertaking* that enhances civilian life (via aesthetics, etc.).
	- Sitte is opposed “street-streets”; small, winding streets among the principles codified.

**The surgery of Paris**

- **Baron von Haussmann (1850s–1860s):** appointed by Napoleon Bonaparte to remake Paris in light of a new emperor and era, in particular one that would not revolt again; known for replacing medieval Paris’ form with the boulevards known today.
	- “The infected body of the city required urgent surgery to resolve the health and social problems of the time.”
	- Sitte’s work is largely a response to Haussmann’s work in Paris, which Sitte saw as “throwing out” the traditional principles of city form.
- Haussmann followed the principles of **axial order** and **geometric unity**, hearkening back to civil order and political stability from before the 19th century.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ The boulevards leading to the Arc de Triomphe, each capped with a monument to represent forms of power in the state (e.g., St. Augustine Cathedral).
	- *Example.* $\quad$ Uniform cornice lines on buildings (iconic!), which all developers must follow—imposes order, but also facilitates entry of real estate entrepreneurs (instead of the state being responsible for building).
	- **Beauxarts:** a highly influential design style known for its ostentation, including radial boulevards and symmetry, plazas.
- The surgery of Paris had significant consequences for social life:
	- Restructuring of roads had political motivation: previously, protestors could easily *barricade* the streets; the wider boulevards were both harder to block and also enabled straightforward deployment of regiments to counter protesters.
	- Many civilians were displaces, pushed to slums on the city extremities.
	- Guidelines for buildings facilitates real estate development and consumptive lifestyle of the bourgeoisie: new sidewalk cafes and shopping malls.
- Ultimately, pushback came from elites who thought Haussmann had gone too far.

**City Beautiful in the United States**

- **City Beautiful:** a campaign to remodel U.S. cities after the European cities that had undergone “Haussmann-ization”; motivated by wanting to achieve cultural, economic, and artistic *parity* with Europe.
	- “Beautiful and clean cities attract desirable citizens. … Clothes don’t make the man, but they come pretty near making the city.”
	-  *Example.* $\quad$ **World’s Fair, Chicago (1893):** consisted of two parts, the “white city” and the “midway.” 
		- The **white city** was modeled after European cities; ultimately a temporary site made of jute (?) over empty warehouses, a simulacra; also a demonstration of technology, as the first city to be fully electrified.
			- Meant to represent what the city *could* become, in contrast with the actual city of Chicago.
		- The **midway** consisted of booths, etc., much more appealing to civilians; eventually burnt down in a worker’s strike.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ **Washington D.C.**
	- *Example.* $\quad$ **New York City:** Grand Central, NYPL; the planning of NYC generally predates City Beautiful, however.
- **Burnham plan for Chicago:** similar to Haussmann, a plan for formal “surgery” of Chicago that would transform it into a “middle class city”.
	- According to Burnham, because the poor could not buy their own beauty, they should share in the beauty of of the city—but, practically, the plan was intended to encourage real estate development.
	- **Environmental determinism:** social effects can be created (and social problems can be solved) by changing urban spaces (e.g., by designing a city that reflects middle class values, people would become urban class).

**Modernism and the Radiant City**

- **Le Corbusier:** prominent figure in the **Modernist** movement who saw cities as “machines for living”; Corbusier believed that his contemporaneous cities were defunct and could not respond to the needs of modern life, particularly **automobility**.
- **SIAM:** the “interational congress of modern architecture” founded by Corbusier, whose **Athens Charter** attempted to lay out the principles for modern urban design.
- Although Corbusier’s plans are only ever built once (for example, the **Plan Voisin** for Paris was *not* built), his motivating principles are highly influential: cities should be *rationalized* for human use, with people residing in common living units based on the “Modulor”, a rationalized human being.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ His notion of the **Radiant City** is reflected in the form of many world cities: Brasilia, Singapore, Co-Op City in NYC.

**Modernism in New York**

- **Robert Moses:** head of Bridge and Tunnel Authority of NYC, known for **Cross-Bronx Expressway** and **Lincoln Center**; inherited approach to urban renewal from Haussmann and Corbusier (tearing down *functional* infrastructure and replacing them with structures that predominately serve white middle-class).
- **Jane Jacobs:** resident of Greenwich Village, objects to Moses’ plans for the **Lower Manhattan Expressway** which would have paved over Washington Square Park, among other areas.
	- Authored several classics (e.g., *The Death and Life of American Cities*; “Downtown is for People”, a criticism of Lincoln Center) that “weakened the profession” of urban design by calling into question the total power and rationality of urban designers, as well as asking who urban design is for. 

**Sprawl and New Urbanism**

- The **Garden City** was a plan for developing residential areas outside of the industrial city.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ **Levittown, NY:** pre-fabricated buildings.
- **Urban sprawl:** when not only residential structures, but also other businesses, etc. move outside of the city; often seen as the *antithesis* for urban design and planning (although many people like living in sprawl—are urban designers elitist for this?).
- **New Urbanism:** a reaction to (sub)urban sprawl that models suburb plans after traditional villages and neighborhoods.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ The Villages, Florida; **Seaside, Florida** (the set of the Truman Show); **Celebration, Florida**.
	- “Fake community that is instilled by the design of a space”, recalling the environmental determinism of Burnham et al.; the problem is that traditional values do not reflect modern sensibilities (e.g., porches exist but are empty, NIMBY-ism, desire to retreat to areas of isolation).
- **Festival marketplace:** an effect of New Urbanism on cities; an area designed to feel like authentic “main streets” and promote community, but effectively act as shopping malls and feel like Celebration, FL.
	- *Example.*$\quad$ The Grove, FL; South Street Seaport, NY.

## Workshop: Introduction to architectural drawings


**Architectural drawing standards**

- Essential formatting elements of an architectural drawing: **title** (what, where, when, by whom); **scale** (graphical/numerical); **orientation** (i.e., compass).
- Other essential elements: line styles (see PDF).
- Great to have: relative line weights (at least two); people; textures (via differentiable hatches); colors (but preferably just one, not more than two—use only if it makes a point); annotations.one, not more than two—use only if it makes a point); annotations.
	- **Heavy lines:** cuts through materials or objects (e.g., walls).
	- **Light lines:** everything that is not cut through.
