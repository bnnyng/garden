# Week 14, Day 1: Multiple Realizability

---

**Putnam’s multiple realizability**

- [[@1975putnamPhilosophy]]’s demonstrative example: Imagine a square peg that doesn’t fit into a round hole. How do we best explain this?
	- A physicalist explanation: Consider the initial conditions (positions and velocities), solve for equations of motion. With certain initial conditions the solution will involve the peg going through the whole, with others it will not.
	- Putnam’s alternative: Just use Euclidean geometry. The Pythagorean theorem says that if the sides of the square are 1, then the diagonal will be greater than 1 and the square peg would not fit into a round hole of diameter 1.
	- Putnam’s explanation highlights that there are some facts about an event that are more **relevant**.
- Putnam’s **multiple realizability** differs from [[@1974fodorSpecial]].
	- Fodor’s MR: Within a given theory of special science, there are pieces of vocabulary that can be realized by different physical situations.
	- Putnam’s MR: We may have an entirely different fundamental physical theory—of microscopic physical events—but as long as it accommodates the possibility of the event we are interested in explaining, 
- What we want from an explanation is what is **relevant**.
- MR grants an **explanatory autonomy** to special sciences; “they don’t stand or fall on the backs of more fundamental sciences.”

**Tension between science and free will**

- The picture from natural science is in tension with “what kinds of things we are.”
	- The most famous and trivial example: freedom of will. This appears to be in tension with fundamental theories of physics like [[Newtonian mechanics]] (e.g., after Laplace, Boscavitch)?
	- Situation is the same if the fundamental laws of the physical world are **chancy** (e.g., in quantum mechanics).
- Kant: Freedom of will is the “capacity to constitute a law unto ourselves.”
	- Augustine sees giving himself over to God as a freedom from natural laws, in the sense of bodily desires, etc.
	- Kant: “commandments of reason,” as opposed to commandments of natural law.

**Compatibilist and reliabilist responses**

- Hume’s **compatibilist** attitude towards freedom of will takes this to not be a problem at all.
	- Raising my arm of “my own free will” simply means “I raised my arm in accord with what my desires were in that moment about what to do with my arm.”
	- This is compatible with the idea that my will is also determined physically.
	- This is a **pedestrian** definition; the relevant part about killing someone, for example, is whether I desired to kill someone or if I was compelled to do so.
- For Kant, this would not suffice. Kant believes that freedom of will requires a **causal chain** to start internally.
- A more radical position: if you can’t carry out actions independently of physics, then you can’t do science.
	- “If physics is right and governs everything, then beliefs must be determined entirely by causes.” But this feels wrong, because “I know why 1+1=2 because it just couldn’t be otherwise, not because of the positions and velocities of particles 5 minutes ago.”
	- #concept-question  But one believes one is a deterministic machine because of reasons?
	- People operate in the spaces of reasons and causes. If everything is just physics, then we only operate in the space of causes.
- #concept-question  The **reliabilist** position: One could build a computer (purely mechanical devices) that only announce conclusions that are “licensed” by evidence and reasons. 
	- “Doesn’t seem inconsistent to say that you are a system like that.”
	- A more acute challenge: How do you know 1+1=2? “You have some kind of feeling that it couldn’t be otherwise.”
- The **knowledge argument**, i.e., [[Mary's Room disproves a materialist basis for consciousness|Mary's Room]]: Mary may even know all the laws of physics and solve the equations of motion to figure out what is going to happen when she moves from a black-and-white environment to the real world. She can predict her own responses—noises, bodily motions—when she sees colors.
	- The claim of the knowledge argument is that even though she knows in advance everything she will physically do, she will *learn something she didn’t previously know*: what the experience of seeing green is like—the phenomenal **qualia** of seeing green.
