# Week 3, Day 1: Scientific Laws
---

**What kind of necessity is involved in a scientific law?**

- Scientific laws are not **logically necessary.**
	- Logical necessities lead to **contradictions** when false, hence limiting what we can imagine.
	- We can easily imagine what the world would be like if certain scientific laws were false (see: most of science fiction).

**Three “jobs” fulfilled by nomic facts**

- Unlike a **restatement**, scientific laws give the feeling of gaining understanding—more than that something did happen, but why it must have happened given some set of conditions.
	- Often involves some conceptual distance—the more remote, the more satisfying—from the phenomena, fitting into larger patterns of the world.
- Unlike **contingently true** facts, **nomic facts** can be exploited to support **counterfactual claims**.
	- Counterfactual claims have the following structure: “Had \[false antecedent] been the case, \[consequence] would have happened.”
		- *Example.* $\text{ }$ “If I hadn’t knocked over the ink, there wouldn’t be a stain on the ground.”
		- *Example.* $\text{ }$ “If I had dropped by glasses, they would have accelerated toward the earth at $9.8 \ m/s^2$.”
	- Counterfactuals appeal to **regularities** and facts of life that have so-called nomic force.
	- Recurring theme of the class: What does it mean for a counterfactual to be true? How can people think they know the truth value of a counterfactual? By construction, the proposition takes place in an alternate world!
- Only propositions that are “confirmable by their instances” ([[@1983goodmanFact]]) seem like candidates for nomic facts.
	- Confidence in laws increases quickly after seeing only a small number of instances.
	- *Non-example.* $\text{ }$”Are there any volcanic rocks in the universe that are not shaped like Mickey Mouse?” Would be absurd to most people to conclude “no”, since it seems like a contingent fact—a poor candidate.

**A flawed proposal: Armstrong’s Platonic contingencies**

- Underlying premise: Hovering above the world of **particular facts** is a Platonic heaven of “real objects,” or **forms**.
	- *Example.* $\text{ }$ Particular red things appear red in virtue of participating in the Platonic property or form of “redness.”
- Nomic truths at the particulars are there because of contingent relations between forms.
	- *Example.* $\text{ }$ The President is the boss of the Vice President not because of the relations between individuals, but because of the relations between their offices.
- See Van Frassen, “Laws and Symmetry,” for critique.

**Another proposal: Maudlin’s Primitivism**

- **Primitivism** is the view that “you need it when you see it,” hence the concept is not possible to analyze further.

**How literal is the governance metaphor?**

- The **governance image** of laws conveys the feeling that there are certain ways of the world that just “happen to be,” and others that are **commanded** to be true by that which governs the world, a sovereign.
	- Clearly a metaphor, but how can we be literal about it?
	- *Pre-scientific example.* $\text{ }$ It is a *de facto* truth that I don’t stand on my head for 10 hours at a time, but a *nomic claim* that I don’t drive at 100 miles per hour on the freeway.
- In “The Will to Power,” Nietzsche argues that 19th century scientists rely on metaphors to convey the feeling that something has been explained, rather than described: “The fictitious insertion of a will.”
	- He shows that the feelings go away after the metaphors are disavowed, hence science does not actually give any of the insight it claims to have!
