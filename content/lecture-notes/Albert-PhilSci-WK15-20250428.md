# Week 15, Day 1: Structure of scientific history

---

**Previously: Intentionality**

- Review: Broadly resisting the claim that everything is physics—or science.
	- Claims from the special sciences
	- Claims based on features of mentality, agency, or consciousness (e.g., qualia, freedom of will, sensitivity to reasons, first-person facts, intentionality)
- Clarification on **intentionality**, after Putnam: Imagine a scenario where an ant on a beach just happens to leave a trace that looks like Winston Churchill. Is this physical phenomena *about* or *refer to* Winston Churchill?
	- The answer is no, since there is not the right kind of intentionality behind this physical state. Maybe if the ant crawled many times on Winston Churchill’s face and is now reminiscing on its youth.
	- Upshot: What decides what this beach refers to is not just its immediate physical properties, but its history. Thus, the property of being about something is not an **intrinsic** property of any particular physical state.
- “Aboutness” is apparently transparently a thing that is true of our mental states (e.g., Husserl believed this was the distinguishing feature of thought). 
	- Applying the same argument to brains, if we had a brain floating in space with no history, we would not want to say it is about something.
	- Many continental philosophers took this to be an argument against **physicalism**.
- The physicalist may respond by agreeing that aboutness is not an intrinsic physical feature, but that we can identify aboutness by examining the causal history of a particular physical state—a **causal theory of reference** (e.g., Fodor).
	- This has the “jarring” implication that the content of a thought is not a function of your mental states, but a function of the *state of the whole world*.

**Resistance to science**

- Three layers of resistance:
	- Resistance to the “unity” of science, i.e., from the special sciences; believes that not everything is grounded in physics.
	- Resistance to grounding in physics instead of grounding in human selves.
	- Challenging that science has a special epistemic privilege: “Claim for itself a privileged, unique access to what the world is like”; vs. theology, for example.
- Kuhn’s historical argument: Science is not above contingent historical facts. Certain scientific theories and research programs are successful for a number of cultural, sociological, and economic reasons.
	- “Undeniably true,” according to Albert.

**Scientific revolutions, after Kuhn**

- Kuhn argues (apparently evident from examining history) that science falls into **normal** and **revolutionary** periods.
	- Problems that we do not know how to solve are dismissed as **peripheral**; scientific **paradigms** often collapse when these are realized to be deep problems.
- Scientific **crises** are not only intellectual, but economic crisis for the **industry** of science. 
- A new paradigm emerges from the revolutionary period, and people work in that paradigm until the next revolution.
- *Example.* $\quad$ Transition from Ptolemian to Copernican cosmology (?). Transition from Newtonian mechanics to relativity.
- Kuhn also makes a philosophical claim that challenges the idea that the new paradigm is better than the old one in a straightforward **epistemic** sense (e.g., contained the old theory and also predicted more true things).
	- Underlying this is the assumption that there is a body of **raw data** that theories may fit better or worse.
- Kuhn believes that there is no data that is not **theory-laden** (as long as we aren’t saying “blue here now”)—there is no such neutral area for comparing two theories.
	- Related to objections to Popper and falsification, holist doctrines of Quine et al. (if something happens that you don’t expect, you could in principle adjust any part of your theory).
	- Kuhn refers to the **incommensurability** of theories like [[Newtonian mechanics]] and special relativity—they cannot be even compared—e.g., on the basis of having two different definitions of momentum. They are not having a debate about what momentum is, they are just talking about two different things.
	- Response: those theories mean the same thing about position, and they make different predictions about the evolution of position in time.
	- Albert: “Argument is disappointing.”
	- There is a bit of a jump from the obvious fact that all observations are theory-laden to the claim that two theories cannot be compared in any objective way, at least relative to the parts of the theory that are held constant.
- For example, Quine differs from Kuhn in saying that some parts of theory are more readily adjusted than others.
- Current consensus on Kuhn: Brought much-needed attention to the historical embeddedness of scientific evolution that was missing in traditional philosophy of science. 
	- Analogous to the observation that America has never been an actual democracy, but the question of democracy does keep popping up. Science has never been conducted in the way we idealize (in the way that confirmation theory and realism might advise), there exists a notion of what science “ought” to be doing that gives you a place to stand for criticizing current practices.
- Theoretical physics currently doesn’t fit into what Kuhn calls normal science: one essential feature is that there is a **core problem** that people are making **steady progress** on.
	- Theoretical physics is *not* experiencing steady progress on unifying quantum physics and universal gravitation.

**Against method, after Feyerabend**

- Another influential American philosopher of science, **Feyerabend** argues that there is no standard doctrine of **scientific method**.
	- It is unfaithful to scientific practice to systemize practices such as confirmation.
- This is also a way of resisting the claim that there is a distinctly scientific way of approaching the world that is distinct from other approaches in human history.

**Fictitious insertion of will, after Nietzsche**

- Nietzsche also influenced many other critiques of science by the following argument (in the Will to Power): 
	- 19th century physicists laugh at Aristotelean explanations on the basis of things like “basis”, thinking there is a causal explanation in accordance in physical laws—e.g., that is how an egg becomes a chicken.
	- Nietzsche says that the term “law” itself is a relic of civil or divine traditions. How should someone understand a so-called law of science?
	- Goes from **governance** to **descriptivist** image of laws: all we are doing is describing what is happening. 
	- Nietzsche says that a descriptivist image of laws is “giving up” any claim to have explained anything (vs. describing it in some detail).
- Nietzsche argues that science is being **disingenuous** by describing the actions of physics as laws, which reminds people of the action of obeying laws. This gives rise to the *feeling* of having grasped an explanation.
	- A **rhetorical device** of scientific discourse is to invoke “purpose” and then explicitly disavow it.
- People take this as the first example of “**deconstruction**”: examining a text and finding that its real meaning is exactly the opposite of what it purports to mean.
- One can take a **Humean** response: the true character of laws is descriptive, not necessitarian. Science gives a slightly deflated form of explanation that tells you how this phenomenon fits into the entire mosaic.
	- Not a critique of scientific law *per se*, though that’s how Nietzsche regarded it.
- Given this view, science is just another instance of a **totalizing ideology**.
- Upshots:
	- Deconstructive literary criticism: many parts of our language that pretend to be literal are pervasively dependent on metaphor. Literal language is the most deceitful of all, poetic language is less so since it is upfront about the role of figurative language.
