# Week 12, Day 1: Quantum Mechanics and Uncertainty
---

**Experiments in uncertainty**

- No correlation between color values (white vs. black) and hardness/softness of electrons. 
	- **First experiment:** Properties detected using boxes. After input to “color” box, 50 percent come out the white “aperture” and 50 percent come out the black. Same with hardness.
- **Second experiment:** Chaining hardness → color → hardness box. Discard hard electrons and keep soft ones after the first box; discard black and keep white ones after the second. Finally, we observe 50 percent hard and 50 percent soft coming out of the final hardness box.
	- “The action of measuring $y$ messes up $x$.”
	- Observation: Passing electrons through the color box seems to flip half of their hardness/softness.
	- Next questions: What determines which electrons get flipped? Can we build a better color box, which measures color without flipping hardness?
	- If you combine the boxes (sum magnetic fields), you get nothing.
- Conclude that there is something **irreducibly** probabilistic going on (c.f. flipping a coin; over many years, you might be able to flip it such that one side is guaranteed).
	- Most probabilistic events in nature are no longer probabilistic after being observed at a sufficiently “microscopic” level.
- **Third experiment:** Given a “hardness” box, have electrons output at perpendicular trajectories. Then add a reflective surface to each of the trajectories, so that they swap directions of travel and eventually end up as inputs to a “black box.” This black box has the property of outputting an electron of the hardness that it entered with (?).
	- For example, if we feed in a large collection of white electrons, we expect 50 percent to come out hard/soft at the end. We expect a similar situation if we feed in a hard/soft electron; the output is 50–50, white to black.
	- However, if you feed a white electron into the hardness box, it will come out white. Something is as the two properties are re-combined.
- If we just measure color, we have no reliable information about its hardness, and vice versa. We can’t say something like, “This electron is white and soft.” The **uncertainty principle** refers to this “quantum-mechanical incompatibility” between color and hardness.
	- Another uncertainty relation is between position and momentum.
	- Uncertainty relations are predictable using the mathematical formalism of quantum mechanics.
- **Fourth experiment:** Add a “wall” on the track of soft electrons.
	- Prediction: total population of electrons out will be down by 50 percent, but those out are all white. Actual outcome; 50–50  black–white split.
- The **total of nothing box** (**Aranov–Bohm effect**?) has such that any output electron has the same properties as its initial input—it alters neither color nor hardness. Electrons that pass *outside* the box also are unaffected.
	- But if we place it in the “black box” situation, the color is changed in a **deterministic**, predictable way.
	- This experiment shows that we don’t mean uncertainty in an **epistemic** sense, but a **metaphysical sense** (“what could be going on?”).

**What’s at stake in interpretations of quantum mechanics?**

- Famously, Quine thought not even the laws of logic are immune to empirical modification.
	- Putnam interpreted the experimental results this way: to take them seriously would be to alter our laws of logic.
	- “The first time in the history of human investigation in which we’ve been forced empirically to modify our laws of logic.” This was taken seriously as a response for about 15 years, though no longer.
	- For example, Putnam suggested to give up the distributive law: $A \wedge (B \vee C) \equiv (A \wedge B) \vee (A \wedge C)$.
	- (Bohm’s (?) work was ignored, as he was a victim of McCarthyism.)
- The **Copenhagen interpretation** takes asking this question at all (which route does the electron take through the box) is a **category error**; it is a completely meaningless question, like asking “Is the number five married?” or “How many grams does Catholicism weigh?”.
	- But not all instances of asking, “Where is the electron?” are this nonsensical?
