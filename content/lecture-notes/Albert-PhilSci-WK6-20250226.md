# Week 6, Day 2: Induction and Confirmation
---

**Problems of induction**

- [[@1983goodman]] Ch. 3 echoes the **Humean** observation that whatever reasoning we use to come to scientific conclusions, it is not **deductive**: “Such judgements are neither reports of experience nor logical consequences of it” (59).
- Goodman argues that there is no distinction between the **descriptive problem** of what induction *is*, and the **justificatory problem**: “What precisely would constitute the justification we seek?” He makes the following arguments:
	- Induction doesn’t involve knowing the future from the past: “If the problem is to explain how we know that certain predictions will turn out to be correct, the sufficient answer is that we don’t know any such thing.”
	- Induction does not predict future events: “We are asking for prevision rather than philosophical explanation.”
	- Induction does not involve the **probability** of events. Frequentist interpretations of probability return to the first case of knowing the future: “If this means determining how the prediction is related to actual **frequency distributions** of future throws of the die, surely there is no way of knowing or proving this in advance.”
- Goodman’s claim is that the **justificatory problem** is ill formed: “Now obviously the genuine problem cannot be one of attaining unattainable knowledge or accounting for knowledge that we do not in fact have” (62).
- To clarify what justification would entail, Goodman examines the justification behind more familiar principles of **deductive inference**: “The answer lies much nearer the surface. Principles of deductive inference are justified by their conformity with accepted deductive practice” (63).
	- Goodman views the relation between deductive inferences and rules of logic as **circular**, involving **mutual adjustment**: “Rules and particular inferences alike are justified by being brought into agreement with each other. *A rule is amended if it yields an inference we are unwilling to accept; an inference is rejected if it violates a rule we are unwilling to amend*” (64).
	- Deductive logic, like other human practices, are an outcome of **empirical experience**; we have instincts about the rules that “feel good to us” and which rules “tend to work out well.”
	- Note that this view is not accepted by the majority of other philosophers, with the exception of Quine.
- Upshot: only the **descriptive problem** has a coherent answer. That is, under what circumstances is a piece of evidence **confirmatory** or **disconfirmatory** of a particular hypothesis?

**The new riddle of induction**

- Goodman’s example of the fictional predicate “grue” shows that rules of confirmation cannot be purely **syntactical**.
	- A thing examined before time $t$ is grue just in case it is green, as well as unexamined things just in case they are blue.
	- The statements that “all emeralds are green” and “all emeralds are grue” are equally well confirmed by syntax, but intuitively we feel that the first is **well-confirmed** by our experience and the latter is not. Hence, the identical syntax does not explain confirmation—the difference is **semantic**, i.e., in what they are asserting.
	- For example, an emerald we had not examined before $t$ would necessarily also be blue.
- What is the difference between “green” and “grue”? Green has historically been projected with success in **epistemic practice**—green is **well-entrenched**, and grue is not.
