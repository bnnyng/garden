# Week 6, Day 1: Counterfactuals and the “Actual” World
---

**Metaphysics of possible worlds**

- David **Lewis** takes the metaphysics of possible worlds very seriously. According Lewis, **possible worlds** are concrete entities that exist as much as the “actual” one does.
	- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ Suppose a bullet narrowly misses my vital organ. According to Lewis, there is a fact of the matter (i.e., a **truth value**) associated with the claim, “If the bullet had been one inch to the left, I would be dead by now.”
- In contrast, **Goodman** takes the word “actual” to be **indexical**: like the words “I” or “now”, the meaning of “actual” depends on the **context** where it s uttered ([[@1983goodman]]).
- **Nagel**’s position is the opposite extreme from Lewis: according to Nagel, counterfactual claims “illustrate the kinds of **causal** or other **nomic** connections that characterize the actual world.”
	- Goodman’s “**militant actualism**” “shows us much more interesting things than Nagel manages to.”

**Evaluating counterfactuals**

- Goodman attempts to evaluate counterfactual claims entirely in terms of the actual world and its history by translating counterfactuals into **causal statements** about the actual world. 
	- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ The counterfactual claim “If the butter had been heated to $x$ Fahrenheit, it would have melted” is equivalent to the claim about actuality, “Since that butter did not melt, it was not heated to above $x$ Fahrenheit.”
- Goodman’s attempt to characterize counterfactuals in his **actualist framework** faces the **problem of auxiliary conditions** when trying to take into account the background (auxiliary) facts.
	- Goodman initially proposed to take all true statements about the world, and then add the **counterfactual antecedent**. However, this immediately leads to a logical **contradiction**, which allows for the derivation of *any* statement.
		- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ Suppose in the “actual” world a match was not scratched. Then adding the antecedent “the match was scratched” to the collection of true statements about the world leads to a contradiction.
	- Refining the auxiliary conditions (e.g., including only **logically** or **nomically consistent** true statements) lead to similar issues.
- A separate issue with Goodman’s attempt to evaluate counterfactuals is that **auxiliary conditions** are defined to be “what could have been the case” if the **antecedent** were true, which presupposes an understanding of counterfactuals.
- As a response to the problems of Goodman’s evaluation, Lewis gives a **similarity-based account** of counterfactuals: evaluating a counterfactual statement (e.g., “the match was lit”) involves looking at the *possible worlds in which the antecedent is true*, and the truth of the statement is determined by what happens in the possible world that is **closest** to our actual world.
	- If the **consequent** (e.g., “the match would have lighted”) is true in the closest possible world in which the antecedent was true, then the counterfactual is true in our world.
	- However, what makes a world “closest” to ours is unclear.

**Relationship between laws and causation**

- Goodman and Lewis are both **unionists**, meaning they do not believe laws have more nomic force than being patterns in the [[Humean mosaic]].
- Goodman rejects any reference to **causal force** to distinguish between laws and accidental correlations. Rather, laws have a *special confirmability by their instances*—they are **well-confirmed generalizations** based on past **predictive success**.
	- According to Goodman, law-like statements are those that our **epistemic community** have successfully **projected** in the past.
	- Goodman’s “**radically relativist**” view of laws implies that there is no objective fact of the matter about which community’s laws truly reflect underlying metaphysical (i.e., causal) structure. That is, causal structures do not exist independently of the mosaic and our projections.
- According to Lewis’s **best system account**, laws are regularities that appear as **axioms** in a “maximally succinct and informative” system of true propositions about the world.
- “The interrelated problems of dispositions, counterfactuals, and possibles are among the most urgent and most pervasive that confront us today in the theory of science.”
