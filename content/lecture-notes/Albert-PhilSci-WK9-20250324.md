# Week 9, Day 1: Problems with Logical Positivism
---

**Review: What are scientific theories about?**

- In the **logical positivist** view, “understanding what a piece of discourse is asserting” (i.e., what a proposition is about) means precisely knowing what observations you need to make to decide whether it is true or false.
- When science is about something that is not **directly observable** (e.g., mechanics), there are two possibilities: either the statement is “gibberish” and refers to mere correlations, or it can be *redefined* in terms of observable phenomena (e.g., the statement “an electron is over here” can be restated in terms of oscilloscopes and spark chambers).
	- Note that the the redefining case, positivists take the very *meaning* of the statement to be its redefinition. 
	- Meaningful scientific statements must have **observable procedures of predication.**
- **Analytic claims** are true by definition (as a classic example, “all bachelors are unmarried”). According to positivists, a statement in terms of oscilloscopes and spark chambers is analytic in the sense that it does not give new information about the world, only information about our use of language.
- The task of **philosophy** is to comb through human discourse and find and clarify the meaningful statements. Afterwards, science empirically discovers which statements are true and which are false.

**The language of direct observation**

- Positivists had varying proposals for the language of direct observation, or *what* observations are needed to know if a statement is true.

| Proposal Name              | Definition                                                                                                                               | Benefits                                                                                                                                                                                   | Problems                                                                |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Phenomenalist              | Locutions should be about internal **sense-data** experiences, e.g., “blue here now” (see Carnap, “The Logical Structure of the World”). | Answers to the problem of **Cartesian skepticism**.                                                                                                                                        | We do not have an expressive language of sense data—won’t get anywhere. |
| ”Ordinary physical things” | Observable features include medium-sized objects (?) like tables and chairs, pointers on measuring devices.                              | Answers to the problem of **under-determination of theory by experiment** (“a more modest form of skepticism”), since positivists take experiment to be the *only* meaning of a statement. | See below example.                                                      |

- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ A statement like “the rat is **hungry**” is not in terms of ordinary physical things, despite being theoretically important. A **logical** translation like “the rat is hungry just in case it will immediately eat food I give it” implies that the rat is not hungry when in the absence of food, which seems untrue.

**The downfall of positivism**

- Quine’s critique of positivism is its problems with **holism**: there is no way to turn a statement like “there is an electron over there” into a **finite** set of propositions about observables.
	- Making definitions truly precise would implicate all facts of the world: “Our beliefs face the tribunal experience not one at as a time, but as a corporate body.”

**[[@1951quineTwo|"Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (Quine, 1951)]]**

- The distinction between **synthetic** and **analytic** is absolutely necessary for positivism. In particular, the relation between a statement about time and a hand on a clock, for example, must be analytic—true by definition.
	- Logic and mathematics are analytic by virtue of being tautologies, which are not statements about the world.
	- Knowing that observable procedures of predication and facts of the world must also be analytic.
- Quine sees beliefs as a **web**: when some observations violate our expectations, the beliefs at the edge are empirical data that are more *willingly* altered. Thus, the difference between central analytic beliefs and contingent beliefs is one of *degree*.
	- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ There will be no circumstance under which we change our belief in “all bachelors are unmarried”—we will not see a news article that meaningfully says “married bachelor discovered on remote island.”
- Quine argues that the corpus of beliefs is tested as a *body*, not individually—the problem of holism for positivism. Logically, we have arbitrarily many choices for changing our previous theory to accommodate an unexpected observation.
	- *Example.* $\ \text{}$ If I see that an apparently straight pencil appears bent when put in a cup of water, I could develop a theory of optics (as science does), or I could conjecture that my tactile sense experience differs in water, etc.
