# Week 3, Day 2: The Humean Mosaic and the Best System View
---

**Last time: [[Albert-PhilSci-WK3-20250203|Week 3, Day 1]]**

- What is **nometicity**? What does it mean for something to be a **scientific law**?
	- **Platonic:** Laws at the level of particulars are contingencies among high-level, abstract forms.
	- **Primitivistic:** Laws are irreducible properties that **govern** our world.
- Scientific laws certainly are not **logically** necessary; we can easily imagine a world where certain facts about the world are not true.
	- Hume asks, “What other kinds of necessity could there be?”

**The “best system” view, after Hume and Lewis**

- In contrast to the governance view, the **descriptivist** view of laws argues that there is no **metaphysical** distinction between laws and other propositions.
- Why bother make this distinction? What makes certain propositions special?
	- Laws are true propositions—taken from the **Humean mosaic**, or totality of all facts and properties in four-dimensional space-time—that jointly maximize **concision** and **informativeness**.
	- Calling something a law means just means that it deserves a “spot on the T-shirt”; note that these laws don’t give us knowledge of particulars, which depend on complicated **initial conditions**.
	- *Question.* $\text{ }$ Does this hold for the “special” sciences (as opposed to fundamental sciences, like physics)?
- What if there exist no simple patterns in the mosaic?
	- Assuming a **deterministic**, **Newtonian world**, the “exceptionlessly true”, T-shirt-worthy facts might include $F = MA$, Coulomb’s Law, Law of Universal Gravitation, etc. 
	- A **chaotic** or **anarchic** world would not accommodate the scientific enterprise—but given the success of science so far, this appears not to be the case.
- The third option is a **probabilistic** world in which laws are not required to be exceptionally true, but more like useful [[Coarse-graining|coarse-grainings]] of phenomena.
- The Humean view gives a more sophisticated meaning to [[Frequentist, subjectivist, and primitivist interpretations of a probabilistic locution|frequentist probabilities]]: frequentist claims are about probabilistic laws “at the bottom”, which best summarize the *entire* mosaic.
	- *Example.* $\text{ }$ What does it mean to say “the coin will land ‘heads’ with probability 0.6”? Frequentist claims are about **fundamental objects** present in the whole mosaic—electrons, protons, etc.—which then gives meaning to the looser, “trivial” claims about composite objects like the coin.
	- Contrast with our first, “vulgar” conception of frequentist probabilities (e.g., the limiting ratio of some infinite number of events).
