**Skyscraper technology**

- Prior to skyscrapers, buildings were built with load-bearing **masonry**; the key innovation that enabled skyscrapers was **steel frames**, which were significantly lighters.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ Home Insurance Building, Chicago, 1885; weighed a third of other buildings in Chicago.
- **Glass curtain walls** are not load-bearing, but obscure the internal structure of the building; associated with the “**international style**.”
	- NS: “The form no longer reflects the function.”
	- *Example.*$\quad$ Seagram Building, New York; United Nations Building, New York, 1951; Lever House, New York, 1952.

**Forces behind the skyline**

- Placement of skyscrapers is constrained by geology.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ Boston, where form is determined by location of bedrock, forming the “spine” of skyscrapers.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ New York has two clusters of skyscrapers—midtown and downtown—that are not “filled in” between them; “if we ever had an earthquake, the skyscrapers would probably be okay, but probably not the brownstones.”
- The most important force is the *economy*: developers build skyscrapers primarily to capture rent → skyscrapers gather in the middle of the city.
	- **Agglomeration:** people become more economically productive through their proximity to other people; cuts down on transportation costs, easier to switch between jobs (e.g., moving to a company where you can be more productive), learning from peers.
	- Rent is more expensive in the city center because it is an **amenity** due to agglomeration; urban economists think that this is the only reason cities exist.
	- *Example.* $\quad$Chicago.

**Zoning codes and skyscraper construction**

- City Beautiful → **City Functional**, the idea that one should be able to study the city analytically and then come up practical solutions.
	- Paradigmatic outcome: **zoning**, e.g., by Fifth Avenue (?) Association concerned by new features (electric signs, garment manufacturing) that would devalue their property and ruin the “character of the neighborhood.”
- Two predominant forms of buildings: the ziggurat, which has a stepwise, layered appearance; and Lever House, which has vertical structure built on a subset of a larger plot of land, allowing circulation of air between buildings.
- Skyscraper construction is as much about air as it is about building: “the skyline as much about sky as it is about line.”
	- Recall first lesson on figure-ground: zoning codes almost treat the sky as figure.
	- **Air rights:** the amount of air that a building can “take up”; city allows developers to trade and sell air rights.
		- *Example.* $\quad$ Small churches in Upper Manhattan that have tall buildings beside them; UTS and JTS have either grouped together their air rights, or sold them to condo developers.
- *Example.* $\quad$ Billionaire’s Row “needle towers”, only possible because lots beside them have sold their air rights to allow them to be incredibly tall.
	- Also requires new technological innovations—top floors have counterweighting **ballast** that oppose forces of wind and stabilize the building.
	- Initial construction also inspired a moral panic, similar to the Equitable Building.
