# Week 2, Day 1: Scientific Explanations
---

![[@1966hempel#^3d52d7]]

**What is a good scientific explanation?**

- The ultimate, extreme fantasy of a good scientific explanation is an explanation in which the only **explanans** are the **rules of logic**. 
- This is never the case for natural sciences. Instead, what we often consider “good” is when we have “explained something such that you **understand** that it is true.”
- According to [[@1966hempel]], there are two basic requirements for an explanation to be scientific:
	- **Explanatory relevance:** “The explanatory information adduced offers good grounds for believing that the phenomenon to be explained did, or does, indeed occur” (note that exactly what kind of connection Hempel has in mind is unclear).
	- **Testability:** “Statements constituting a scientific explanation must be capable of empirical test.”
- **Deductive-nomological (DN) explanations** involve applying formal [[Deductively valid inferences are necessarily truth-preserving as a matter of form alone|deductive]] logic to *physically observable and testable* **explanans**, or premises. 
	- According to [[@1966hempel]], DN explanations satisfy the [[Scientific explanations must be explanatorily relevant and testable, after Hempel|requirement of explanatory relevance]] in “the strongest sense,” since the explanans give logically conclusive ground for expecting the **explanandum phenomenon**. 
	- DN explanations must include at least one **law**—merely contingent facts do not give a satisfying explanation.

**[[Laws of nature|Natural laws]], after Hempel**

- Laws feel different from facts in that they **avoid particulars**. They play crucial roles in explanation, but are often **implicitly assumed** rather than explicitly stated.
- Albert claims laws of biology and chemistry are **non-fundamental**, relative to physics.
- The current discussion of natural laws is **metaphysical**; from an **epistemic** standpoint, we may never be certain of them.
