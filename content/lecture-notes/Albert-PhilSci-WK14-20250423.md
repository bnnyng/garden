# Week 14, Day 2: More ways of resisting reductionism
---

- Review: Different ways of resisting the claim of **reductionism**—that there’s nothing in the world aside from physics.
	- [[@1975putnamPhilosophy]] and [[@1974fodorSpecial]] argue on the basis of the relationship between physics and the **special sciences**.
	- Another form of resistance comes from agency, freedom of will, and phenomenal experience (i.e., **qualia**).
- [[@1982jacksonEpiphenomenal]]’s **Knowledge Argument**: 
- People like Jackson do not deny the **causal closure of the physical world**—the view that there is a thoroughly physical explanation will suffice to explain an unambiguously phenomena; conversely, the physical explanation will never take you outside the realm of the physical.
	- The **libertarian** (as opposed to Humean **compatibilist**) view of **freedom of will** would deny this.
	- Recall: Compatibilists view freedom of will is *doing* as one wills—it doesn’t matter if someone cannot *will* as they will.
	- The Kantian libertarian stance is that there is a causal origin for actions within oneself not encoded in some physical state. (Unclear whether Kant believes in the causal closure of the physical world.)

**Third person limitations**

- David Lewis’ proposed argument against **physicalism**: in the world, there are two brains that have identical physical states that both know precisely everything in the world as well as its entire physical history (in the **third person**). Further, there exists a rock that will fly towards one of the brains, e.g., the one on the right, and hit it. Lewis argues that the brains would still wonder, “Am I the brain that will be hit?”
	- Note that the question has to be raised with an **indexical**, i.e., first person language.
		- *Question.* $\quad$ Does the kind of fact that they are about—subjective—make this question somehow suspect?
	- The implication is that when one agent “sees” the rock heading towards it, it will learn something new.
	- A more prosaic example: You might have a *complete* map that does not settle where you are located; you learn something new by an “X” marking your location.

**Intentionality**

- Brain states seem to have **intentionality**—they are *about* something. How could this aboutness relation come (which can be between two physical objects, e.g., my brain and a table) in a physical way? *What* is this aboutness relation?
	- **Husserl** took intentionality to be the fundamental feature of mental activity—what distinguishes it from merely physical events.

**Scientific revolutions**

- All individuals we’ve discussed agree that while there are puzzles about how science works, it is obvious that science is a **unique epistemic project** that is distinct from all other human epistemic projects (e.g., theology, philosophy).
	- It is distinguished by its epistemic **success**; no other human epistemic project comes close to it.
- An alternative view is that what is interesting about science is *why* it gets this epistemic privilege.
- In “The Structure of Scientific Revolutions,” Kuhn emphasizes that as a human activity, science is much more continuous with other activities—culture, politics, etc.—than the others we’ve discussed believed, even radical skeptics like Goodman and Quine.
	- Scientific inquiry is influenced by cultural, economic, political, etc. influences.
	- The scale at which this affects scientific **consensus** is much larger than what the analytic philosophers we’ve discussed acknowledge.
- Kuhn notes that there is an ongoing psychological game that individuals and societies are playing of distinguishing between important and unimportant problems.
	- The “unimportant” problems are actually ones that the scientific communities do not have the tools to solve; instead, they tell themselves that the problem is “trivial,” etc.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ The history of quantum mechanics: people convinced themselves that there was “nothing serious going on” in certain core questions.
	- *Example.* $\quad$Physicians ignoring the complaints of women.
	- *Example.* $\quad$ Curare, originally a paralytic agent, was thought by Westerners to also be an anesthetic—and thus used in surgery.
